A language model that has read the internet knows that a sponge goes next to the sink and that "the red block" is the one that is red. A robot arm needs more than that: it must turn a camera image and an instruction into motor commands, many times per second, for long enough to finish the job. **Vision-language-action models** are the current attempt to get both at once, by starting from a pretrained vision-language model and teaching it to output actions.

Almost all such models are trained by imitation: humans teleoperate the robot, and the model learns to predict what the human did. This works remarkably well, and it is also the reason reinforcement learning re-enters the picture. A cloned policy drifts into situations the demonstrator never showed it, inherits the demonstrator's mistakes, and never learns from its own successes. This section describes what a VLA is, how actions are represented, why imitation is not enough, and the main ways RL fine-tuning is done: in simulation, on real hardware, offline, and for the diffusion and flow-matching action heads that many current models use.

## What a VLA is

<Definition name="Vision-language-action model">
A **vision-language-action model** (VLA) is a policy $\pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t, \ell)$ whose network is initialized from a pretrained vision-language model. At each control step it receives an observation $o_t$, consisting of one or more camera images and usually the robot's **proprioceptive state** (joint angles, gripper opening, end-effector pose), together with a natural-language instruction $\ell$, and it outputs an action $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$ or a short sequence of actions. The action space is typically end-effector displacements and a gripper command, or joint targets.
</Definition>

The point of the pretrained backbone is transfer. Every demonstration costs a person's time on real hardware, so even the largest robot datasets are tiny next to the image–text corpora a VLM is trained on. Starting from a VLM gives the policy object recognition, spatial vocabulary and some common sense for free; the robot data only has to teach the mapping to motion.

Three systems mark out the design space.

- **RT-2** (Brohan et al., 2023) introduced the name. It fine-tunes large VLMs on robot trajectories, writing each action as a string of discrete tokens so that the model's ordinary next-token output head produces actions. Training mixes robot data with the original web vision-language data (co-fine-tuning), so the model keeps its general knowledge.
- **OpenVLA** (Kim et al., 2024) is an open-source model in the same mould: a 7B-parameter VLM built on a Llama 2 language model with fused DINOv2 and SigLIP visual features, trained on the Open X-Embodiment collection of robot datasets, again with actions as discrete tokens.
- **$\pi_0$** (Black et al., 2024) keeps a VLM backbone (PaliGemma) but adds a separate **action expert**: a smaller set of transformer weights that attends to the VLM's internal representations and generates a whole chunk of continuous future actions by **flow matching**, rather than token by token.

The difference between the first two and the third is the main design choice in the field: whether actions are tokens or continuous vectors.

## Representing actions

### Discretized tokens

The simplest way to make a language model output an action is to make the action a sentence. Fix per-dimension bounds $[\ell_d, u_d]$, often taken as low and high quantiles of the training data so that outliers do not waste resolution, and a number of bins $B$. Each coordinate is mapped to an integer
$$
b_d = \min\!\left( \left\lfloor B \, \frac{a_d - \ell_d}{u_d - \ell_d} \right\rfloor,\; B - 1 \right), \qquad d = 1, \dots, D,
$$
after clipping $a_d$ to $[\ell_d, u_d]$, and each integer is assigned a token in the vocabulary. RT-2 and OpenVLA both use $B = 256$ bins per dimension. The policy is then an autoregressive categorical distribution,
$$
\pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t, \ell) = \prod_{d=1}^{D} \pi_\theta\!\left(b_{t,d} \mid o_t, \ell, b_{t,1}, \dots, b_{t,d-1}\right),
$$
trained with the usual cross-entropy loss. Two consequences matter for RL. First, the log-likelihood $\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t, \ell)$ is an exact, cheap sum of token log-probabilities, so every algorithm from Sections 3–5 applies unchanged: PPO ratios, GRPO advantages and KL penalties are computed exactly as for text. Second, the action is decoded one token at a time, which limits the control frequency and quantizes fine motion.

### Compressed tokens

Binning each dimension at each time step produces many highly correlated tokens: consecutive actions of a smooth trajectory are nearly equal. **FAST** (Pertsch et al., 2025) instead tokenizes a whole chunk of actions at once. It applies a discrete cosine transform along time to each action dimension, quantizes the coefficients (most high-frequency ones become zero), and compresses the resulting integer sequence with byte-pair encoding. Smooth chunks then need few tokens, and the model still has an exact token-level likelihood.

### Continuous heads

The alternative is to keep actions continuous and use a generative model for them. A Gaussian head is the classical choice, but demonstrations are often **multimodal**: around an obstacle, half the operators go left and half go right, and a Gaussian fitted to both averages them into a collision. **Diffusion Policy** (Chi et al., 2023) showed that a denoising diffusion model over action sequences, conditioned on the observation, captures such multimodality well. Flow matching, as in $\pi_0$, learns a velocity field $v_\theta(x, \tau; o_t, \ell)$ that transports Gaussian noise to the action distribution along an ordinary differential equation; sampling integrates that ODE in a handful of steps. Section 9 develops both model families in detail.

### Action chunking

Most modern policies predict not one action but a **chunk** $a_{t:t+H} = (a_t, \dots, a_{t+H-1})$ of $H$ future actions, executed wholly or partly before the policy is queried again. The idea was popularized by ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) and is used by Diffusion Policy, $\pi_0$ and FAST. Chunking reduces the number of decisions per episode, smooths the motion, and copes better with demonstrations that pause or hesitate.

| | Binned tokens | Compressed tokens | Continuous head (diffusion / flow) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Output | one token per dimension per step | a few tokens per chunk | real-valued chunk |
| Multimodality | yes (categorical per token) | yes | yes |
| Exact log-likelihood | yes | yes | no (see below) |
| Decoding cost | many sequential tokens | fewer tokens | a few network evaluations |
| Off-the-shelf RL from Sections 3–5 | directly | directly | needs modification |

## The robot as a decision process

A robot task is not an MDP in the clean sense of Section 1, and the differences shape every algorithm below.

**Partial observability.** The true state $s_t$ includes object poses, masses, friction, occluded parts of the scene and the contents of closed drawers. The policy sees only $o_t$, so the problem is a POMDP. Most VLAs condition on the current observation or a short history, and fail when the relevant information is not visible, for instance which of two identical cups was already filled.

**Control frequency and horizon.** Policies act at rates from a few hertz to tens of hertz, so a task lasting a minute is hundreds to thousands of decisions. With discount $\gamma$ per step, the effective horizon $1/(1-\gamma)$ has to be long, which makes value estimation hard.

**Chunks as macro-actions.** If the policy outputs a chunk of length $H$ and executes it open-loop, the decision process seen by the learner is a semi-MDP: decisions are made at $t = 0, H, 2H, \dots$, the "action" is the whole chunk, its reward is $\sum_{j=0}^{H-1} \gamma^j r_{t+j}$, and the next state is discounted by $\gamma^H$. The Bellman equation for the chunk-level action value is
$$
Q(s_t, a_{t:t+H}) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sum_{j=0}^{H-1} \gamma^j r_{t+j} + \gamma^H V(s_{t+H}) \right].
$$
This divides the number of decisions by $H$, which helps credit assignment and value bootstrapping, at the cost of reacting to new observations only every $H$ steps.

**Sparse success rewards.** The natural reward is a success indicator: $r_t = 1$ at the step the task is completed, after which the episode ends, and $r_t = 0$ otherwise. If $T_{\mathrm{succ}}$ denotes the success time (infinite on failure), then
$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[ \gamma^{T_{\mathrm{succ}}} \right] = \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \, \Pr_{\pi_\theta}(T_{\mathrm{succ}} = t),
$$
so the discounted objective rewards both succeeding and succeeding quickly, while with $\gamma = 1$ and a finite horizon it is just the success rate. Such a reward carries at most one bit per episode, which is why exploration from scratch is hopeless and every practical method starts from an imitation-trained policy.

## Why imitation is not enough

**Behaviour cloning** fits $\pi_\theta$ by maximum likelihood on demonstration pairs $(o_t, a_t)$ collected by an expert policy $\pi^*$. It is supervised learning, and its guarantee is a supervised one: small error on the states the expert visits. The policy, however, is run on the states that *it* visits, and those differ once it makes a mistake. This mismatch is called **covariate shift**, and its cost grows with the horizon.

<Result name="Compounding errors in behaviour cloning">
Consider a horizon-$T$ problem with per-step costs in $[0, 1]$ and a deterministic expert $\pi^*$, and let $J$ denote expected total cost. Let $\epsilon_t$ be the probability that the learned policy $\hat\pi$ chooses a different action from $\pi^*$ at a state drawn from the expert's state distribution at step $t$, and let $\epsilon = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \epsilon_t$. Then
$$
J(\hat\pi) \le J(\pi^*) + T^2 \epsilon .
$$
</Result>

*Proof.* Run $\hat\pi$ and $\pi^*$ from the same initial state with the same environment randomness. The two trajectories coincide until the first step at which $\hat\pi$ chooses a different action. Up to that step the shared trajectory is distributed as an expert trajectory, so the probability that the first disagreement happens at step $k$ is at most $\epsilon_k$, and the probability that it happens at or before step $t$ is at most $\sum_{k=0}^{t} \epsilon_k$. If no disagreement has occurred by step $t$, the costs at step $t$ are equal; otherwise they differ by at most $1$. Hence the expected costs at step $t$ differ by at most $\sum_{k \le t} \epsilon_k$, and summing over $t$,
$$
J(\hat\pi) - J(\pi^*) \le \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \sum_{k=0}^{t} \epsilon_k \le T \sum_{k=0}^{T-1} \epsilon_k = T^2 \epsilon . \qquad \blacksquare
$$

The quadratic factor is the formal version of "small errors compound": a mistake at step $k$ can put the robot in an unfamiliar state where it keeps making mistakes for the rest of the episode. Examples exist in which this bound is essentially attained, so it is not an artifact of the proof.

The remedy proposed by Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011) is to train on the learner's own state distribution. In **DAgger**, the current policy is rolled out, the expert labels the states it visits with the actions it would have taken, the labelled states are added to the dataset, and the policy is retrained on the aggregate. The reason this helps is clearest from the performance difference identity, which for a horizon-$T$ problem with costs reads
$$
J(\hat\pi) - J(\pi^*) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_t^{\hat\pi}}\!\left[ Q_t^{\pi^*}\!\big(s, \hat\pi(s)\big) - V_t^{\pi^*}(s) \right],
$$
where $d_t^{\hat\pi}$ is the learner's state distribution and $Q_t^{\pi^*}$, $V_t^{\pi^*}$ are the expert's cost-to-go functions. The bracket vanishes when $\hat\pi(s) = \pi^*(s)$. If a single deviation followed by expert play costs at most $u$ extra, the bracket is at most $u$ times the indicator of a disagreement, so
$$
J(\hat\pi) - J(\pi^*) \le u \, T \, \tilde\epsilon, \qquad \tilde\epsilon = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \Pr_{s \sim d_t^{\hat\pi}}\!\big(\hat\pi(s) \ne \pi^*(s)\big).
$$
The error $\tilde\epsilon$ is now measured on the learner's own distribution, and DAgger's analysis, via no-regret online learning, shows that, after enough iterations, at least one policy in the sequence has $\tilde\epsilon$ close to the best error achievable by the policy class on the aggregated data. The resulting guarantee is linear in $T$ (times $u$) rather than quadratic. The constant $u$ is small exactly when the task is **recoverable**, that is, when the expert can fix a mistake quickly.

For robots this analysis says three things. Errors compound, so demonstrations must cover recovery from off-nominal states, which human teleoperators rarely produce on their own. Fixing this requires interaction: the policy has to visit its own mistakes. And the target is still the expert, so a policy trained this way can at best match the demonstrator, including its hesitations, its idiosyncratic strategies and its failures. Reinforcement learning addresses all three: it learns on-policy, it can learn recovery from reward alone, and it optimizes the task outcome rather than agreement with a person.

## Where rewards come from

RL needs a reward, and in robotics there is no checker as clean as a unit test.

- **Simulator success signals.** In simulation the full state is known, so success is a predicate on it: the block is inside the bowl, the drawer is open. This is the closest analogue of the verifiable rewards of Section 5, and it is where most RL fine-tuning of VLAs has been done.
- **Learned success detectors.** On real hardware one trains a binary classifier on images labelled success or failure, often from a modest number of examples per task, and uses its output as the reward. A pretrained VLM can serve the same role when prompted with the instruction and the final image. Like the reward models of Section 4, such detectors can be exploited: a policy may learn to produce images that look like success.
- **Human signals.** A person can label success at the end of each episode, or intervene during it; an intervention marks the preceding behaviour as bad and supplies corrective actions.
- **Progress estimators.** To densify a sparse reward, one can learn a model that predicts how far along the task an observation is, for example from the time index within successful demonstrations, and use its increments as shaped reward. This helps exploration but can again be gamed. If the shaped reward has the potential-based form $\gamma \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)$ for a function $\Phi$ of the state, the optimal policy is unchanged; other forms of shaping carry no such guarantee.

## RL fine-tuning of VLAs

All current approaches start from an imitation-trained policy and use RL to improve it, usually with a constraint that keeps it close to that starting point. They differ in where the data comes from.

### Online RL in simulation

With token-based VLAs and a simulator, the recipe of Section 5 transfers almost verbatim. The prompt is the initial observation and instruction, the response is the episode, the token-level policy is the product formula above, and the reward is the success indicator. Because a simulator can be reset to exactly the same initial configuration, group-relative methods are natural: draw an initial state $s_0 \sim \mu$, roll out $K$ episodes from it with returns $G^{(1)}, \dots, G^{(K)}$, and use
$$
\hat A^{(i)} = \frac{G^{(i)} - \operatorname{mean}_j G^{(j)}}{\operatorname{std}_j G^{(j)}}
$$
as the advantage of every action token in episode $i$, inside a clipped PPO-style objective with ratios $\rho_t(\theta)$. Alternatively one trains a value head and uses PPO with generalized advantage estimation, as in Section 3. As in Section 5, groups in which all episodes succeed or all fail give zero advantage and no signal, so the curriculum of initial states matters.

Several 2025 papers follow this pattern, including SimpleVLA-RL and VLA-RL, which apply GRPO- or PPO-style updates to token-based VLAs on simulated manipulation benchmarks. The broad qualitative findings are that RL from sparse success rewards improves a reasonable imitation policy, particularly on states and object configurations under-represented in the demonstrations, and that it can discover behaviours absent from the demonstrations. How much of this transfers to real robots, and how it compares with simply collecting more demonstrations, is not yet settled.

<Remark>
The reset to an identical initial state is what makes group baselines cheap. In the real world no two episodes start in exactly the same configuration, so a group of $K$ rollouts does not share an initial state and the group mean is only an approximate baseline. Real-world methods therefore lean on learned critics instead.
</Remark>

### Real-world RL

On a physical robot every episode costs minutes of wall-clock time, a person may have to reset the scene, and exploration can break things. The methods that work here are **off-policy actor–critic** algorithms that reuse every transition many times and seed the replay buffer with demonstrations. SERL (Luo et al., 2024) packages such a pipeline, built around a sample-efficient off-policy learner that mixes offline demonstration data with online data, a learned image-based success classifier as reward, and reset-free training in which a forward policy and a backward (reset) policy are learned together. HIL-SERL (Luo et al., 2024) adds human interventions during training, which both prevent damage and inject corrective data. These systems train comparatively small task-specific policies rather than full VLAs, but they establish that real-world RL on contact-rich tasks is feasible within hours of interaction.

For a large VLA, updating all its weights online is usually too slow and too risky. A common compromise is a **residual policy**: keep the base policy $\pi_{\mathrm{base}}$ frozen and learn a small policy that outputs a correction,
$$
a_t = a_t^{\mathrm{base}} + \delta_\psi(o_t, a_t^{\mathrm{base}}), \qquad a_t^{\mathrm{base}} \sim \pi_{\mathrm{base}}(\cdot \mid o_t, \ell).
$$
The residual sees an MDP whose dynamics include the base policy, so any off-policy RL algorithm applies to it; initializing $\delta_\psi$ near zero means training starts from the base policy's behaviour, and bounding $\delta_\psi$ limits how far exploration can stray.

### Offline RL and reward-weighted imitation

The cheapest option uses no new interaction at all beyond logged rollouts: collect episodes from the current policy (and possibly earlier ones), label them with rewards, and update by a weighted form of imitation. The simplest version, **filtered behaviour cloning**, keeps only successful episodes and clones them. A principled generalization comes from the KL-regularized objective of Section 4. Maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[A(s, a)] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid s))$ over all distributions $\pi$ gives
$$
\pi^\star(a \mid s) = \frac{1}{Z(s)} \, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a \mid s) \exp\!\left( \frac{A(s, a)}{\beta} \right).
$$
Projecting $\pi^\star$ onto the parametric family by minimizing $\mathrm{KL}(\pi^\star \,\|\, \pi_\theta)$ yields a weighted maximum-likelihood problem on samples from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$:
$$
\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{s,\, a \sim \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\!\left[ \exp\!\left( \frac{A(s, a)}{\beta} \right) \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \right].
$$
This is **advantage-weighted regression** (reward-weighted regression when returns replace advantages). It needs only $\log \pi_\theta$ of logged actions, never a ratio or a sample from $\pi_\theta$, which makes it attractive for large models and for continuous heads whose training loss is a stand-in for the log-likelihood. Filtered BC is the limit in which the weight is an indicator of success.

A related idea is to **condition** rather than weight: add the advantage, or a binary "this action was good" flag, as an extra input during training, and at deployment condition on the good value. The model learns from all data, including failures, and the conditioning steers it towards the better part of its distribution.

Finally, one can leave the policy untouched and learn a critic $Q_\phi(o, a)$ by offline RL on logged data, then use it at deployment: sample several candidate actions or chunks from the frozen VLA and execute the one with the highest $Q_\phi$. Because the candidates come from the policy, the critic only has to rank actions the policy already considers plausible, which avoids the worst failure of offline RL, an overestimated value on actions far from the data.

### Searching with a world model

Picking the best of $N$ sampled actions by $Q_\phi$ judges each candidate by one number, the critic's estimate of what follows. A **world model** $M_\psi(s' \mid s, a)$, trained to predict the next state, can look further ahead. The classical ways of using one in RL train on its predictions: generate imagined transitions and add them to the replay buffer, or optimize the policy or value function inside imagined rollouts. On long-horizon, visually complex robot tasks the model's errors then flow into the policy and the critic and compound. Dong, Jia, Finn and Sadigh (2026) take the opposite route in **QWM** (Q-Learning with World Models): the policy and the critic are trained only on real transitions, by an ordinary off-policy Q-learning algorithm, and the world model is used only to **choose actions**, by a short tree search, both when collecting online data and at evaluation.

The tree alternates state and action layers. At a state node at depth $d$, draw $N$ candidate actions $a_d^{n} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_d)$; at each action node, query the world model $M$ times for next states $s_{d+1}^{n, m} \sim M_\psi(\cdot \mid s_d, a_d^{n})$; stop at a shallow depth $D$. Each node then gets two estimates of its value:
$$
V_Q(d \mid s_d) = \operatorname*{agg}_{n} Q_\phi(s_d, a_d^{n}), \qquad
V_r(d \mid s_d) = \operatorname*{agg}_{n} \operatorname*{agg}_{m} \Big[ r_\psi(s_d, a_d^{n}) + \eta\, V(d + 1 \mid s_{d+1}^{n, m}) \Big],
$$
where $\operatorname{agg}$ is an aggregation over the sampled children, $r_\psi$ a learned reward, and $\eta$ a discount on the tree's lookahead. At the last layer the value is just $\operatorname{agg}_n Q_\phi(s_D, a_D^n)$ over freshly sampled actions. The first estimate trusts only the critic and ignores the imagined future; the second uses the imagined future but inherits the model's errors, more of them the deeper it recurses. QWM averages them, $V(d \mid s_d) = \tfrac12 \big( V_Q(d \mid s_d) + V_r(d \mid s_d) \big)$, and scores each root action the same way,
$$
Q_{\mathrm{ts}}(s_0, a_0^{n}) = \tfrac12 \Big( Q_\phi(s_0, a_0^{n}) + r_\psi(s_0, a_0^{n}) + \eta\, \operatorname*{agg}_{m} V(1 \mid s_1^{m}) \Big), \qquad s_1^m \sim M_\psi(\cdot \mid s_0, a_0^n).
$$
The executed action is a max or softmax over the $N$ root candidates under $Q_{\mathrm{ts}}$. In the sparse-reward manipulation tasks of the paper no reward model is learned, so the $r_\psi$ terms drop out and the search is driven entirely by the critic evaluated at imagined states. The tree grows like $N^D M^D$, so it is pruned as a beam: at each depth only the $J$ partial paths with the largest discounted sum $\sum_{d'} \eta^{d'} Q_\phi(s_{d'}, a_{d'})$ are expanded.

The authors build QWM on top of two off-policy learners, EXPO and RLPD, with two kinds of world model: a residual MLP predicting low-dimensional states, and, for pixel observations, a pretrained text-and-image-to-video generator (Wan2.2) fine-tuned on demonstrations to predict short future clips conditioned on robot actions. The second is the kind of video model whose RL fine-tuning Section 9 discusses, here repurposed as a simulator. They report gains in sample efficiency over the base learners on simulated manipulation benchmarks, from both state and pixel observations. Their ablations favour using the search in both data collection and evaluation, a shallow tree, and a small lookahead discount $\eta$.

<Remark>
QWM is a reminder of two facts from Section 2. First, it sits at the boundary between model-free and model-based RL: the model is used for **decision-time planning** only, never as a source of training targets, which is why its errors cannot accumulate in $Q_\phi$. They can still mislead the search, so the design keeps rollouts short and weights the imagined future lightly. Second, choosing the maximum of many noisy estimates is biased upwards, the maximization bias of Section 2. The authors observe that very large $N$ can hurt and attribute it to world-model and value errors being amplified across more candidates; more candidates give the search more chances to find an action that is overrated rather than good. Selecting among actions the policy proposes, rather than over the whole action space, limits the damage in the same way as critic-guided selection above.
</Remark>

## RL for diffusion and flow action heads

For a token-based VLA the log-likelihood is a sum of log-softmax values. For a continuous head it is not available, and this breaks the policy-gradient algorithms of Section 3, which all need $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$.

To see why, consider a flow-matching head sampled by the ODE $\frac{dx}{d\tau} = v_\theta(x, \tau; o, \ell)$ from Gaussian noise to an action chunk. The map from noise to action is deterministic, so the density of the action is the pushforward of a Gaussian through that map. By the instantaneous change-of-variables formula, its log-density is the Gaussian log-density of the initial noise minus the integral of the divergence $\nabla_x \cdot v_\theta$ along the trajectory. This is computable in principle, but it requires integrating a Jacobian trace through the whole sampling path and differentiating through it, which is expensive and noisy; and with the few, coarse integration steps used for fast control, the formula for the continuous ODE does not even describe the sampler actually being run. There are two families of fixes.

### Make denoising stochastic: a two-layer MDP

Replace each deterministic integration step by a Gaussian one. Following Section 9, write the denoising chain from noise $x_T$ to the executed action chunk $x_0$; here $T$ counts denoising steps, not robot time steps, and we index it by $k$. Each step becomes
$$
x_{k-1} = \mu_\theta(x_k, k; o_t, \ell) + \sigma_k \xi_k, \qquad \xi_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
$$
with $\sigma_k \gt 0$ either fixed or learned. Each transition now has an explicit Gaussian density, and the probability of the whole chain is the product of these densities. Treat the chain as an **inner MDP** nested inside the robot's **outer MDP**: at robot step $t$, the inner state is $(o_t, x_k, k)$, the inner action is $x_{k-1}$, the inner reward is zero until $k = 0$, and at that point the chunk $x_0$ is executed and the outer reward and next observation arrive. The policy gradient for the combined process is
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sum_{t} \hat A_t \sum_{k=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta\!\left(x^{(t)}_{k-1} \mid x^{(t)}_{k}, k; o_t, \ell\right) \right],
$$
where $x^{(t)}$ is the denoising chain at robot step $t$ and every inner step of that chain shares the outer advantage $\hat A_t$. PPO applies with per-step ratios $\rho = p_\theta / p_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ for each inner transition. DPPO (Ren et al., 2024) develops this approach for diffusion policies, and Section 9 uses the same two-layer view, together with the conversion of a flow-matching ODE into a stochastic differential equation with the same marginals, to fine-tune image generators.

The price is a longer effective horizon (robot steps times denoising steps), extra variance from the injected noise, and the need to keep $\sigma_k$ small enough that the stochastic sampler still produces good actions.

### Use critics or noise-space policies

The second family avoids action likelihoods altogether.

- **Critic-guided selection.** Learn $Q_\phi(o, a)$ and pick the best of several sampled chunks, as described above. The policy itself does not change.
- **Backpropagating a critic through the sampler.** Treat the sampler as a differentiable function $a = f_\theta(o, x_T)$ of the noise and maximize $\mathbb{E}[Q_\phi(o, f_\theta(o, x_T))]$ by the reparameterization trick, in the style of deterministic policy gradients, usually together with the original denoising loss as a regularizer. This needs gradients through every denoising step but no densities.
- **Noise-space policies.** Freeze the generative head and train a small policy $\pi_\psi(w \mid o)$ that chooses the initial noise $w$ fed to it, so that the executed action is $a = f(o, w)$. From the point of view of $\pi_\psi$, the frozen sampler is part of the environment, and $w$ is an action in an ordinary MDP with an ordinary Gaussian policy, so any standard RL algorithm applies. Because every $w$ is mapped through the pretrained sampler, the executed actions stay close to the support of the demonstrations, which acts as a built-in trust region.

Which of these works best for large VLAs is an open question; the two-layer formulation is the most general, while the critic-based and noise-space methods are cheaper and leave the base model intact.

## Safety, resets and the sim-to-real gap

On real hardware the cost of an episode is not only time. Exploration can collide with the environment, damage objects or the robot, and leave the scene in a state that a person must restore. Practical systems therefore restrict exploration: workspace limits and force thresholds that end an episode, small residual or noise-space policies with bounded outputs, KL or trust-region constraints to the base policy, and human interventions that both stop unsafe behaviour and become training data. **Resets** are a separate bottleneck: a method that needs hundreds of episodes from varied initial configurations needs someone, or a learned reset policy, to produce them.

Simulation removes these costs but introduces the **sim-to-real gap**. Rendering differs from camera images, and contact, friction and deformable objects are modelled only approximately. A policy optimized against a simulator will exploit its inaccuracies exactly as a policy optimized against a learned reward model exploits that model's errors (Section 4). Standard mitigations are domain randomization of visual and physical parameters and simulated scenes built to reproduce the real one closely. The VLA backbone helps with the visual part of the gap, since pretrained visual features are fairly robust to appearance changes, but it does not help with dynamics.

| | Imitation (BC) | RL in simulation | RL on real hardware |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data | human demonstrations | unlimited simulated rollouts | robot's own episodes, plus demos |
| Reward | none (match expert) | exact success predicate | learned detector or human labels |
| Main cost | teleoperation time | building faithful simulations | robot time, resets, risk |
| Typical algorithm | maximum likelihood | PPO or GRPO, on-policy | off-policy actor–critic, residual, offline weighting |
| Learns recovery | only if demonstrated | yes | yes |
| Can exceed demonstrator | no | yes | yes |
| Main failure mode | compounding errors | sim-to-real gap, simulator exploitation | sample cost, reward-detector exploitation |

## Open problems

- **Credit assignment over long horizons.** Success rewards arrive after hundreds of steps. Chunking helps, as do learned progress rewards, but neither is a general solution, and the interaction between chunk length and value learning is poorly understood.
- **Rewards that generalize.** A success detector per task does not scale to a generalist policy. VLM-based judges are general but noisy and exploitable, and how to make them reliable reward signals is open.
- **What RL adds.** As for the reasoning models of Section 5, it is unclear how much RL creates new capabilities versus sharpening behaviours already present in the imitation policy. Comparisons with equal-effort data collection are rare.
- **RL at scale on real robots.** Current real-world RL successes involve single tasks or small task families. Fine-tuning a generalist VLA across many tasks and robots with real interaction, without eroding its general abilities, is largely unexplored.
- **Best practice for continuous heads.** The two-layer MDP, critic-based and noise-space approaches each have clear trade-offs, but there is no consensus on which to use, or on how to combine RL with the flow-matching training objective.
