A text-to-image model trained by maximum likelihood learns to reproduce its training distribution. That is not quite what its users want. They want images that look good, that contain the three red cubes the prompt asked for, that spell the words on the sign correctly, and that people prefer when shown side by side. None of these targets is a likelihood. Each is easier to *score* than to demonstrate, and that is exactly the situation in which reinforcement learning helps.

This section treats diffusion and flow-matching generators as policies. Sampling is already sequential: a diffusion model turns noise into an image through many small stochastic denoising steps, which can be read as actions in a Markov decision process whose only reward arrives at the end. The tools of Sections 3–5 then apply almost verbatim. There is also a tool with no counterpart for language models: when the reward is differentiable in the image, one can backpropagate through the sampler itself.

## Why fine-tune generators with rewards

A pretrained generator $p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_0 \mid c)$ maps a prompt $c$ to a distribution over images (or videos) $x_0$. Reward fine-tuning maximizes
$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{c \sim p(c)}\, \mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid c)} \big[ r(x_0, c) \big],
$$
usually with a penalty keeping $p_\theta$ close to $p_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Typical rewards fall into a few groups.

- **Aesthetics.** A small regression head on top of a frozen image encoder, trained on human aesthetic ratings, gives a scalar "how good does this look" score that ignores the prompt entirely.
- **Prompt alignment.** Whether the image depicts what the prompt says: the similarity of image and text embeddings, a vision-language model answering questions about the image, or an object detector checking counts, colours and spatial relations.
- **Text rendering.** Run optical character recognition on the image and compare the result to the text requested in the prompt.
- **Human preference.** A reward model trained on pairwise human choices between images for the same prompt, exactly as in Section 4.
- **Toy objectives.** JPEG-compressed file size is cheap, unambiguous and visibly changes the images (flatter, less textured), which makes it a convenient test of whether an algorithm works.

One property decides which algorithms are available. JPEG size, OCR, detectors and VLM judges are **non-differentiable** in the pixels; neural reward models such as aesthetic and preference predictors are **differentiable**, with a cheap $\nabla_{x_0} r(x_0, c)$. Policy-gradient methods need only reward values; reward backpropagation needs the gradient.

## Diffusion and flow models in brief

### Diffusion

A **denoising diffusion probabilistic model** (DDPM) fixes a forward noising process that turns data $x_0$ into approximately Gaussian noise $x_T$ over $T$ steps. With a schedule $\alpha_t \in (0, 1)$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t} \alpha_s$,
$$
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1},\, (1 - \alpha_t) I\big), \qquad q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big).
$$
(We avoid the customary $\beta_t$, reserved here for the KL coefficient.) So $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. A network $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c)$ is trained to predict the noise,
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{simple}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, x_0,\, \varepsilon} \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) \big\|^2 ,
$$
which is a reweighted form of the evidence lower bound (ELBO) on $\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid c)$. Sampling runs the learned reverse chain from $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$:

<Definition name="Gaussian reverse kernel">
$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mu_\theta(x_t, t, c),\, \sigma_t^2 I\big), \qquad \mu_\theta(x_t, t, c) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \Big( x_t - \frac{1 - \alpha_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) \Big),
$$
with a fixed variance $\sigma_t^2$. The model density of a full chain is $p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c) = p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)$.
</Definition>

Two facts about this kernel drive everything below. Its log-density is explicit,
$$
\log p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) = -\frac{\| x_{t-1} - \mu_\theta(x_t, t, c) \|^2}{2 \sigma_t^2} + \text{const},
$$
so every step has a tractable log-probability and score function. And it is reparameterizable: $x_{t-1} = \mu_\theta(x_t, t, c) + \sigma_t z_t$ with $z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, so the final image is a differentiable function of $\theta$ for fixed noise. The marginal $p_\theta(x_0 \mid c)$, an integral over all chains, is not available.

Deterministic samplers such as DDIM set the per-step noise to zero; the chain is then a deterministic map from $x_T$ to $x_0$ with no per-step log-probability.

### Flow matching and rectified flow

**Flow matching** learns a time-dependent velocity field instead of a noise predictor. Here time is continuous; we use the convention $t \in [0, 1]$ with data at $t = 0$ and noise at $t = 1$, matching the diffusion indexing. The **rectified flow** (linear) interpolation is
$$
x_t = (1 - t)\, x_0 + t\, \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),
$$
and a network $v_\theta(x_t, t, c)$ is regressed onto the conditional velocity $\frac{d}{dt} x_t = \varepsilon - x_0$:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, x_0,\, \varepsilon} \big\| (\varepsilon - x_0) - v_\theta(x_t, t, c) \big\|^2 .
$$
The minimizer is the marginal velocity $v_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon - x_0 \mid x_t = x]$, and the ODE $\frac{dx}{dt} = v_t(x)$ transports the marginals $p_t$ of $x_t$ into each other. Sampling integrates it from $t = 1$ to $t = 0$ with a few Euler steps; given $x_1$ the image is fixed, which is awkward for policy gradients.

## Denoising as a Markov decision process

<Definition name="Denoising MDP">
Fix a diffusion model with $T$ steps and a reward $r(x_0, c)$. The **denoising MDP** has

- state $s_k = (c,\, t,\, x_t)$ with $t = T - k$, so the episode runs from $t = T$ down to $t = 1$;
- action $a_k = x_{t-1}$;
- policy $\pi_\theta(a_k \mid s_k) = p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)$;
- deterministic transitions $s_{k+1} = (c,\, t - 1,\, x_{t-1})$;
- initial distribution $\mu$: $c \sim p(c)$, $t = T$, $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$;
- reward $r(s_k, a_k) = r(x_0, c)$ at the last step (when $t = 1$) and $0$ otherwise, with $\gamma = 1$.
</Definition>

The trajectory of this MDP is the sampling chain, its return is $r(x_0, c)$, and $J(\theta)$ above is its expected return. The policy is Gaussian with explicit log-probability, so the machinery of Section 3 applies without change. Unlike the token MDP of Section 4, actions are continuous and high-dimensional (a whole latent image) and the horizon is fixed by the sampler. The reward is extremely sparse: nothing says which of the fifty denoising steps made the cubes red.

## Policy gradients over the sampling chain

### DDPO

Black et al. (2023) introduced **denoising diffusion policy optimization** (DDPO), which applies the likelihood-ratio gradient to the denoising MDP.

<Result name="Policy gradient for the denoising chain">
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{c,\; x_{0:T} \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid c)} \Big[ r(x_0, c) \sum_{t=1}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) \Big].
$$
</Result>

The derivation is the usual one. Write $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_c \int p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c)\, r(x_0, c)\, dx_{0:T}$, which is legitimate because $p_\theta(x_0 \mid c)$ is the marginal of the chain and the reward depends only on $x_0$. Differentiating under the integral and using $\nabla_\theta p = p\, \nabla_\theta \log p$,
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_c \int p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c)\, r(x_0, c)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c)\, dx_{0:T}.
$$
Since $\log p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c) = \log p(x_T) + \sum_{t} \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)$ and $p(x_T)$ does not depend on $\theta$, the gradient of the chain log-density is the sum of per-step scores. The intractable marginal never appears.

Each per-step score is explicit:
$$
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) = \frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}\, \big( \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(x_t, t, c) \big)^{\!\top} \big( x_{t-1} - \mu_\theta(x_t, t, c) \big) .
$$
It pushes the predicted mean towards the drawn sample in proportion to the final reward. As in Section 3, $r$ is replaced by an advantage $\hat A = r - b(c)$ with a baseline; DDPO normalizes rewards using per-prompt statistics.

This plain estimator needs fresh samples for every gradient step. The importance-sampled variant reuses each batch for several updates, as PPO does. With chains sampled from $p_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ and the per-step ratio
$$
\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)}{p_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)},
$$
the surrogate is the clipped PPO objective summed over denoising steps,
$$
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}} \Big[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \min\big( \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat A,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\, \hat A \big) \Big].
$$
Every step of a chain shares the same $\hat A$. Using per-step ratios instead of the product $\prod_t \rho_t$ is accurate only near $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$, which clipping enforces.

Black et al. demonstrated DDPO on compressibility, incompressibility, an aesthetic predictor, and prompt alignment judged by a vision-language model. They also observed reward overoptimization.

### DPOK and the KL penalty over the chain

Fan et al. (2023) proposed **DPOK**, which adds the KL regularization of Section 4:
$$
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_c \Big[ \mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid c)}\, r(x_0, c) - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big( p_\theta(x_0 \mid c) \,\|\, p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_0 \mid c) \big) \Big].
$$
The KL between image marginals is intractable for the same reason the marginals are. It can, however, be bounded by a sum of per-step terms.

<Result name="KL upper bound over the chain">
If both chains start from the same $p(x_T)$, then
$$
\mathrm{KL}\big( p_\theta(x_0 \mid c) \,\|\, p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_0 \mid c) \big) \le \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid c)} \Big[ \mathrm{KL}\big( p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) \,\|\, p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) \big) \Big].
$$
</Result>

*Proof.* By the chain rule $\mathrm{KL}(p(a, b) \,\|\, q(a, b)) = \mathrm{KL}(p(a) \,\|\, q(a)) + \mathbb{E}_{p(a)} \mathrm{KL}(p(b \mid a) \,\|\, q(b \mid a))$, marginalizing cannot increase KL, so the left side is at most $\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c) \,\|\, p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{0:T} \mid c))$. Applying the chain rule along the Markov chain, the $x_T$ term vanishes because both chains start from the same noise distribution, and each remaining step contributes the expected KL of its transition kernels. $\square$

For Gaussian kernels with a shared variance the per-step term is closed form,
$$
\mathrm{KL}\big( p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) \,\|\, p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c) \big) = \frac{\| \mu_\theta(x_t, t, c) - \mu_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_t, t, c) \|^2}{2 \sigma_t^2},
$$
so the regularizer is a cheap distance between the two networks' predictions along chains sampled from the current model. It is the diffusion analogue of the per-token KL penalty of Section 4. DPOK used the ImageReward preference model as its reward and found the KL term important for preserving quality and diversity.

## Backpropagating the reward through the sampler

When $r$ is differentiable, there is a lower-variance route. Fix the initial noise $x_T$ and the per-step noises $z_1, \dots, z_T$. The sampler is then a deterministic function $x_0 = F_\theta(x_T, z_{1:T}, c)$ obtained by composing $T$ network evaluations, and
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{c,\, x_T,\, z_{1:T}} \Big[ \big( \partial_\theta F_\theta(x_T, z_{1:T}, c) \big)^{\!\top} \nabla_{x_0} r(x_0, c) \Big].
$$
This pathwise gradient uses the direction in which the reward improves, not just its value, and works for deterministic samplers: DDIM or a flow ODE is just a $F_\theta$ with no $z$ terms.

The cost is memory and stability: backpropagating through $T$ steps stores $T$ copies of a large network's activations and multiplies $T$ Jacobians. Three related methods handle this differently.

- **DRaFT** (Clark et al., 2023) backpropagates through the full chain with gradient checkpointing and LoRA adapters, and introduces **DRaFT-K**, which truncates the backward pass to the last $K$ denoising steps ($x_K$ is treated as a constant); even $K = 1$ works well.
- **AlignProp** (Prabhudesai et al., 2023) similarly uses LoRA and checkpointing, truncating the backward pass after a randomly chosen number of steps.
- **ReFL** (reward feedback learning), introduced with ImageReward by Xu et al. (2023), samples to a randomly chosen late step $t$, forms the one-step estimate $\hat x_0$ from $x_t$, and backpropagates the reward of $\hat x_0$ through that single prediction, mixed with the ordinary denoising loss as a regularizer.

Truncation makes the gradient biased: the early steps, which decide composition and layout, receive no signal, so the methods are better at changing texture, colour and style than global structure. The large and accurate gradient is also a liability. Following a reward network's pixel gradient finds its adversarial directions quickly. **Reward hacking** shows up sooner and more severely with reward backpropagation than with policy gradients; early stopping, KL or weight-space regularization towards the reference, and interpolating fine-tuned with reference weights are common defences.

## Diffusion-DPO

Direct preference optimization (Section 4) skips the reward model and trains directly on pairs $x_0^w \succ x_0^l$ for a prompt $c$. For diffusion the needed $\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid c)$ is intractable, so Wallace et al. (2023) work at the level of whole chains and then make a sequence of approximations. We sketch the argument, writing each approximation explicitly.

**Step 1: move to the chain.** Define the objective over chains $x_{0:T}$ instead of images, with a chain reward $R(c, x_{0:T})$ whose expectation over chains ending in $x_0$ is $r(x_0, c)$, and the KL penalty on the joint distribution:
$$
\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_c \Big[ \mathbb{E}_{x_{0:T} \sim p_\theta}\, R(c, x_{0:T}) - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big( p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c) \,\|\, p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{0:T} \mid c) \big) \Big].
$$
*Approximation 1:* the joint KL upper-bounds the image KL (the result above), so this is a stronger regularizer than the one in the original problem.

**Step 2: DPO on chains.** Exactly as in Section 4, the optimum is $p^*(x_{0:T} \mid c) \propto p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{0:T} \mid c) \exp(R(c, x_{0:T}) / \beta)$, so the reward can be written in terms of the chain log-ratio, and the Bradley–Terry likelihood of a preference pair becomes a function of $\beta \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T} \mid c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{0:T} \mid c)}$ evaluated on chains ending in $x_0^w$ and $x_0^l$. Since the data contain only final images, the log-ratios are averaged over $x_{1:T} \sim p_\theta(x_{1:T} \mid x_0, c)$.

**Step 3: Jensen.** The expectation sits inside $\log \sigma$. Because $-\log \sigma$ is convex, moving the expectation outside gives an upper bound on the loss. *Approximation 2:* minimize this bound.

**Step 4: replace the reverse posterior with the forward process.** Sampling $x_{1:T}$ from $p_\theta(x_{1:T} \mid x_0, c)$ is intractable. *Approximation 3:* use the forward noising process $q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)$ instead. This is the substitution that underlies the diffusion ELBO.

**Step 5: reduce to denoising errors.** The chain log-ratio is a sum of $T$ per-step log-ratios; replace the sum by $T$ times one uniformly sampled step. For each step, the expected log-ratio under $q$ satisfies the exact identity
$$
\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)} \Big[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)} \Big] = \mathrm{KL}\big( q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x_t, c) \big) - \mathrm{KL}\big( q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_t, c) \big).
$$
Each KL is between Gaussians with matched variances, and in the noise parameterization it equals $w_t \| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) \|^2$ up to an additive constant, with $w_t \gt 0$ a schedule-dependent weight and $\varepsilon$ the noise used to form $x_t$ from $x_0$. The constants cancel between the two KLs. The result:

<Result name="Diffusion-DPO loss">
With $x_t^w = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0^w + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon^w$ and $x_t^l$ formed likewise from independent noises,
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(c, x_0^w, x_0^l),\; t,\; \varepsilon^w,\, \varepsilon^l} \log \sigma\Big( -\beta T w_t \big[ \Delta_\theta^w - \Delta_\theta^l \big] \Big),
$$
$$
\Delta_\theta^w = \| \varepsilon^w - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t^w, t, c) \|^2 - \| \varepsilon^w - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_t^w, t, c) \|^2 ,
$$
and $\Delta_\theta^l$ defined the same way for the losing image.
</Result>

The loss decreases when the trained model improves on the reference more for the winner than for the loser; the loser term is what pushes the model away from dispreferred outputs. In practice $w_t$ is often taken constant and absorbed into $\beta$.

The loss is an upper bound on a bound on the DPO loss for a stronger regularizer, evaluated on forward-process chains the model never samples. It is fully offline and cheap, needing one denoising pass per image; Wallace et al. trained Stable Diffusion XL on the Pick-a-Pic dataset of human pairwise preferences. Like DPO in Section 4, it cannot explore beyond the dataset.

## Flow-GRPO: policy gradients for flow models

Flow-matching models sample by integrating an ODE, so there is no per-step distribution and no likelihood ratio. J. Liu, G. Liu and colleagues (NeurIPS 2025) made GRPO (Section 5) applicable to them with **Flow-GRPO** ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05470), [project page](https://gongyeliu.github.io/Flow-GRPO/), [code](https://github.com/yifan123/flow_grpo)), whose central step is to replace the ODE by a stochastic differential equation with the same marginals.

### From ODE to SDE

<Result name="SDEs with the ODE's marginals">
Let $p_t$ be the marginals generated by $\frac{dx}{dt} = v_t(x)$, and let $\sigma_t \ge 0$ be any noise schedule. Then the SDE
$$
dx = \Big[ v_t(x) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t(x) \Big] dt + \sigma_t\, dW
$$
run in the direction of increasing $t$ has the same marginals $p_t$. Its time reversal, run from $t = 1$ to $t = 0$ (so $dt \lt 0$, with $\bar W$ a Brownian motion in reversed time), is
$$
dx = \Big[ v_t(x) - \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t(x) \Big] dt + \sigma_t\, d\bar W,
$$
and also has marginals $p_t$.
</Result>

*Proof sketch.* The marginals of the ODE satisfy the continuity equation $\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (p_t v_t)$. The SDE's marginals satisfy the Fokker–Planck equation
$$
\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot \Big( p_t v_t + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} p_t \nabla \log p_t \Big) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \Delta p_t = -\nabla \cdot (p_t v_t),
$$
because $p_t \nabla \log p_t = \nabla p_t$ and $\nabla \cdot \nabla p_t = \Delta p_t$. The two equations coincide, so started from the same distribution they produce the same marginals. The reverse form follows from the standard time-reversal formula for diffusions, which subtracts $\sigma_t^2 \nabla \log p_t$ from the forward drift. $\square$

The score is not learned separately: for the rectified-flow interpolation it is a function of the velocity. Since $x_t = (1 - t) x_0 + t \varepsilon$ is a Gaussian perturbation of $(1 - t) x_0$ with standard deviation $t$, Tweedie's formula gives $\nabla \log p_t(x) = -\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t = x] / t$. Taking conditional expectations of $x = (1 - t) x_0 + t \varepsilon$ and of $v_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon - x_0 \mid x_t = x]$ and eliminating $\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_t = x]$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t = x] = x + (1 - t)\, v_t(x)$, hence
$$
\nabla \log p_t(x) = -\frac{x + (1 - t)\, v_t(x)}{t}, \qquad 0 \lt t \le 1 .
$$

### The stochastic policy

Replacing $v_t$ by $v_\theta$ and discretizing the reverse SDE with Euler–Maruyama and step $\Delta t \gt 0$ gives the update from $x_t$ to $x_{t - \Delta t}$:
$$
x_{t - \Delta t} = x_t - \Big[ v_\theta(x_t, t, c) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2t} \big( x_t + (1 - t)\, v_\theta(x_t, t, c) \big) \Big] \Delta t + \sigma_t \sqrt{\Delta t}\; z, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
$$
This is a Gaussian policy $\pi_\theta(x_{t - \Delta t} \mid x_t, c)$ with mean given by the bracketed drift and covariance $\sigma_t^2 \Delta t\, I$, exactly the structure of the DDPM kernel. With $\sigma_t = 0$ it reduces to the Euler ODE sampler. Too little noise kills exploration; too much degrades samples at a fixed step count. Flow-GRPO takes
$$
\sigma_t = a \sqrt{\frac{t}{1 - t}},
$$
with a single scalar noise level $a$ as a hyperparameter, so the injected noise is largest early in sampling (near $t = 1$, where the formula is capped in practice) and vanishes as $t \to 0$, where small perturbations would show up directly as visible artefacts.

### Group-relative advantages

Flow-GRPO then runs GRPO as in Section 5. For each prompt $c$, sample $K$ images $x_0^{(1)}, \dots, x_0^{(K)}$ with the SDE sampler, score them, and form
$$
\hat A^{(i)} = \frac{r(x_0^{(i)}, c) - \operatorname{mean}_j\, r(x_0^{(j)}, c)}{\operatorname{std}_j\, r(x_0^{(j)}, c)} .
$$
Every step of chain $i$ uses $\hat A^{(i)}$ in the clipped per-step surrogate, with a closed-form Gaussian KL penalty to the reference. No critic over noisy latents is needed.

A practical observation in the same paper is **denoising reduction**: training-time sampling can use many fewer steps than inference-time sampling. Rollouts are worse, but the within-group ranking still drives learning and the gains transfer to the full sampler.

### Models and tasks

All the main experiments fine-tune a single base model, **Stable Diffusion 3.5 Medium**, a rectified-flow text-to-image transformer, on three tasks chosen to cover three kinds of reward:

- **Compositional generation**, scored by the GenEval rules: an object detector checks that the image contains the requested objects with the right counts, colours and spatial relations, and the reward is computed from those checks. This is the closest image analogue of a verifiable reward (Section 5).
- **Visual text rendering**, for prompts that ask for a given string to appear in the image. An OCR model reads the image and the reward is one minus the edit distance between the recognized and the requested text, normalized by the length of the request.
- **Human preference alignment**, with PickScore, a learned model of which of two images people prefer for a prompt, as the reward. This is the RLHF-style setting of Section 4, with all of its exposure to reward hacking.

Each task trains a separate model. Besides the task reward, the authors track image-quality and preference scores the policy was not trained on, to detect reward hacking, and they report that the KL penalty to the reference is what keeps those scores from degrading as the task reward rises. The released code has since added other base models (FLUX.1-dev, Qwen-Image, Wan2.1 for video) and faster variants that inject noise at only one or two steps of each chain, but the paper's claims rest on the SD3.5-Medium experiments.

## DanceGRPO

**DanceGRPO** (Z. Xue et al., ByteDance Seed and the University of Hong Kong, 2025; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07818), [project page](https://dancegrpo.github.io/), [code](https://github.com/XueZeyue/DanceGRPO)) appeared at almost the same time as Flow-GRPO and arrives at the same core idea, a stochastic sampler plus group-relative advantages. Its emphasis is different: one formulation for both diffusion and rectified-flow models, several base models at once, and above all **video**.

### Diffusion and rectified flow as SDEs

For a diffusion model with forward SDE $dx = f_t x\, dt + g_t\, dW$, DanceGRPO samples with the family of reverse-time SDEs
$$
dx = \Big( f_t x - \frac{1 + \eta_t^2}{2}\, g_t^2\, \nabla \log p_t(x) \Big) dt + \eta_t\, g_t\, d\bar W ,
$$
which all share the marginals $p_t$: $\eta_t = 0$ is the deterministic probability-flow ODE and $\eta_t = 1$ the standard reverse-time SDE. For rectified flow it uses
$$
dx = \Big( v_t(x) - \frac{\eta_t^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t(x) \Big) dt + \eta_t\, d\bar W ,
$$
the reverse SDE of the result above with $\sigma_t = \eta_t$. The score is again computed from the network's output, through the predicted clean sample $\hat x_0 = x_t - t\, v_\theta(x_t, t, c)$: $\nabla \log p_t(x_t) \approx -(x_t - (1 - t)\, \hat x_0)/t^2$, which is algebraically the same expression as the Tweedie formula above. In the released code the noise scale is a constant, so each Euler–Maruyama step has standard deviation $\eta \sqrt{\Delta t}$. Either way every denoising step is a Gaussian policy with a closed-form log-probability, and the denoising MDP of this section applies unchanged to both model families.

### The algorithm

The update is the clipped per-step surrogate with group-relative advantages, with three design choices worth noting.

- **Shared initial noise.** All $K$ samples for a prompt start from the same $x_T$ (for flow models, the same noise at $t = 1$), so they differ only through the noise injected along the way. The authors report that this makes training more stable and less prone to reward hacking. A plausible reading: with independent starting noise, much of the reward variation within a group comes from the starting point rather than from anything the policy did along the chain. The README of the released code adds a caveat: if the reward model cannot tell apart trajectories that start from the same noise, varying the starting noise within a group can be necessary.
- **Multiple rewards, normalized separately.** With reward models $R_1, \dots, R_M$, each reward is standardized within the group and the results are summed,
$$
\hat A^{(i)} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{r_m^{(i)} - \mu_m}{\sigma_m},
$$
where $\mu_m$ and $\sigma_m$ are the mean and standard deviation of $R_m$ over the group. This is the combination VGGRPO uses below, and for the same reason: no reward dominates because of its units.
- **Timestep subsampling.** Only a random fraction $\tau$ of the $T$ denoising steps of each chain enter the loss, $\lceil \tau T \rceil$ of them. This cuts the cost of a policy update roughly in proportion; the authors report that training on a subset of steps loses little.

The released training code has no KL penalty to the reference and uses a very small clip range, so the trust region comes from the clipping alone.

### Models, tasks and rewards

DanceGRPO is evaluated on three tasks and four base models:

- **Text-to-image** with Stable Diffusion v1.4 (a diffusion model), FLUX.1-dev (rectified flow), and HunyuanVideo used as a text-to-image model.
- **Text-to-video** with HunyuanVideo.
- **Image-to-video** with SkyReels-I2V, where the reward targets motion quality, since the given first frame largely fixes the appearance.

The rewards cover five kinds of signal: image aesthetics (HPS-v2.1, a human-preference model), text–image alignment (CLIP score), video visual quality and video motion quality (both from the VideoAlign video reward model), and binary rewards obtained by thresholding a score. The image results illustrate the multi-reward advantage: training on the preference model alone raises its score but can lower text–image alignment, while adding CLIP score as a second reward keeps both up, a small instance of the reward-hacking trade-offs discussed below. Training on video uses the same algorithm, with a rollout being a full video; the released code offers the option of generating a large group and training only on its highest- and lowest-reward members, which keeps the most informative contrasts while reducing the cost of the update.

### Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO side by side

| | Flow-GRPO | DanceGRPO |
|---|---|---|
| Model families | rectified flow | diffusion and rectified flow |
| Stochastic sampler | reverse SDE, $\sigma_t = a \sqrt{t/(1-t)}$ | reverse SDE family with scale $\eta_t$ |
| Main base models | SD3.5-Medium | SD v1.4, FLUX.1-dev, HunyuanVideo, SkyReels-I2V |
| Tasks | compositional T2I, text rendering, preference | T2I, T2V, I2V |
| Rewards | GenEval rules, OCR, PickScore | HPS-v2.1, CLIP score, VideoAlign, binary |
| Saving compute | fewer denoising steps in training | subset of steps in the loss |
| Group noise | independent | shared initial noise |
| Regularization | KL penalty to reference | clipping (no KL in released code) |

Both papers confirm the point of this section: once the sampler is stochastic, a flow model is an ordinary policy, and the LLM machinery of Section 5 carries over with little change. Their differences are engineering answers to the same questions — how much noise to inject, how to save compute on long chains, and how to keep a learned reward from being gamed.

## Three families compared

| | Policy gradient (DDPO, DPOK, Flow-GRPO) | Reward backpropagation (DRaFT, AlignProp, ReFL) | DPO-style (Diffusion-DPO) |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the reward must provide | values only | gradient with respect to pixels | pairwise preferences, offline |
| Sampler requirement | stochastic steps with tractable log-probs | differentiable, may be deterministic | none at training time |
| Data | online samples from current model | online samples from current model | fixed dataset of pairs |
| Gradient | unbiased (before clipping), high variance | low variance, biased under truncation | from a chain of approximations |
| Credit assignment over steps | same terminal advantage to every step | exact through differentiated steps | uniform over a sampled step |
| Main risk | sample cost, slow progress | fast reward hacking | limited to the data distribution |

Non-differentiable rewards (OCR, detectors, vision-language judges, compressibility) rule out the middle column; offline preference pairs without a reward model point to the right one.

## Reward models for generation

For language models, Section 5 could often replace the learned reward by a checker: a maths answer is right or wrong. A generated image or video has no correct answer to compare against, so in practice almost every method in this section gets its reward $r(x_0, c)$ from a separate model that looks at the sample and the prompt and returns a number. Which model that is decides what the generator actually learns, so it is worth knowing the main families, how each is built, and what each can and cannot see.

### What a reward model has to provide

Four properties matter for RL.

- **Prompt conditioning.** A reward of the form $r(x_0)$ can only push every prompt towards the same kind of image; prompt alignment needs $r(x_0, c)$.
- **Differentiability.** Reward backpropagation (DRaFT, AlignProp, ReFL) needs $\nabla_{x_0} r$; policy-gradient methods (DDPO, Flow-GRPO, DanceGRPO) need only values, so detectors, OCR and VLM judges qualify.
- **Cost.** Group methods score $K$ samples per prompt at every iteration, so a slow judge dominates training time. This is why VGGRPO moves its geometry reward into latent space.
- **Calibration across prompts.** A score of the same size can mean different things for different prompts. Group-relative advantages subtract the per-prompt mean and divide by the per-prompt standard deviation, so only the ranking of samples within a prompt matters, the same invariance that makes Bradley–Terry reward models usable in Section 4.

### Embedding similarity: CLIP score

CLIP trains an image encoder $E_{\mathrm{img}}$ and a text encoder $E_{\mathrm{txt}}$ contrastively on image–caption pairs so that matching pairs have high cosine similarity. The **CLIP score** of a sample is that similarity,
$$
r_{\mathrm{CLIP}}(x_0, c) = \frac{\langle E_{\mathrm{img}}(x_0),\, E_{\mathrm{txt}}(c) \rangle}{\|E_{\mathrm{img}}(x_0)\|\, \|E_{\mathrm{txt}}(c)\|},
$$
usually rescaled. It is cheap, differentiable and prompt-conditioned. Its weakness comes from how it was trained: a contrastive objective over captions rewards getting the gist right, so the embedding is a poor judge of counts, spatial relations, attribute binding ("a red cube and a blue ball" against the colours swapped) and rendered text. It is better as a secondary reward that keeps samples on-prompt than as the main objective.

### Aesthetic predictors

An **aesthetic predictor** is a small regression head on frozen image embeddings (typically CLIP's), fitted to human ratings of how good images look. The common example is the LAION aesthetic predictor ([code](https://github.com/christophschuhmann/improved-aesthetic-predictor)): a small network on CLIP ViT-L/14 image embeddings, regressed with a squared loss onto average human ratings from photography and AI-art rating datasets (AVA among them). It was built to filter the LAION image collection, and it was the reward in DDPO's aesthetic-quality experiment. It ignores the prompt by construction, which makes it the textbook case of prompt-agnostic collapse: optimized hard, it drives every prompt towards the same lighting, palette and style.

### Human-preference models

The most widely used rewards are **image–text preference models** trained on pairwise human judgements. The recipe is the one of Section 4. Collect prompts $c$ with two generated images, ask people which they prefer, and fit a scorer $r_\phi(x, c)$ under the Bradley–Terry model
$$
P(x_w \succ x_l \mid c) = \sigma\big( r_\phi(x_w, c) - r_\phi(x_l, c) \big)
$$
by maximum likelihood. The models differ in data and backbone.

#### ImageReward

**ImageReward** (Xu et al., NeurIPS 2023; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05977), [code](https://github.com/THUDM/ImageReward)) was the first general-purpose reward model of this kind for text-to-image generation. Its training data are about 137 thousand pairwise comparisons from expert annotators, who ranked several images generated for the same real-world user prompt; each ranking is broken into pairs and fitted with the loss above. The paper introduced it together with **ReFL**, the reward-backpropagation method described earlier in this section, so ImageReward was designed from the start as an optimization target rather than only a metric. It is distributed as a small Python package and remains a standard evaluation score; the same group later released VisionReward, a finer-grained, multi-dimensional successor covering images and video.

#### PickScore

**PickScore** (Kirstain et al., NeurIPS 2023; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01569), [code](https://github.com/yuvalkirstain/PickScore)) comes from **Pick-a-Pic**, a dataset collected through a public web application in which users typed their own prompts, were shown two generated images, and picked the one they preferred (or declared a tie). Because the prompts and choices come from real users rather than paid annotators, the data reflect what people actually ask image generators for. The model is a CLIP ViT-H/14 fine-tuned on these choices, and its score is the scaled image–text similarity
$$
r_\phi(x, c) = \tau \left\langle \frac{E_{\mathrm{img}}(x)}{\|E_{\mathrm{img}}(x)\|},\, \frac{E_{\mathrm{txt}}(c)}{\|E_{\mathrm{txt}}(c)\|} \right\rangle ,
$$
with $\tau$ CLIP's learned temperature. The probability that one image beats the other is a softmax of their scores over the pair,
$$
P(x_1 \succ x_2 \mid c) = \frac{e^{r_\phi(x_1, c)}}{e^{r_\phi(x_1, c)} + e^{r_\phi(x_2, c)}} = \sigma\big( r_\phi(x_1, c) - r_\phi(x_2, c) \big),
$$
which is exactly the Bradley–Terry model; ties are trained towards probability one half. The original model was trained on Pick-a-Pic v1; a later release, Pick-a-Pic v2, has more than a million examples. Pick-a-Pic is also the preference data of Diffusion-DPO, which learns from the same pairs without a reward model, and PickScore is the human-preference reward of Flow-GRPO.

#### HPS v2 and v2.1

**Human Preference Score v2** (Wu et al., 2023; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09341), [code](https://github.com/tgxs002/HPSv2)) is built on the **Human Preference Dataset v2** (HPD v2): about 798 thousand human preference choices over some 430 thousand images, generated by many text-to-image models from a cleaned set of prompts. HPS v2 fine-tunes a CLIP ViT-H/14 on these choices in the same Bradley–Terry fashion. The release also defines a benchmark: a fixed set of evaluation prompts in four styles (animation, concept art, painting and photo), on which models are ranked by average score. **HPS v2.1** is a later checkpoint trained on higher-quality data; it is the main image reward of DanceGRPO, whose released training prompts also come from HPD. Because HPD prompts and styles are what HPS was trained on, optimizing HPS-v2.1 on HPD prompts and then reporting HPS-v2.1 on the HPS benchmark is an especially direct case of training on the evaluator.

These models are prompt-conditioned and differentiable, which is why they serve as both RL rewards and backpropagation targets. They also carry the biases of their annotators, their prompt distributions and the generators that produced their training images, and they are widely observed to over-reward saturated colour, high contrast and dense detail, the first symptoms listed under reward hacking below. A preference model trained on images from older generators is also being asked to judge images from a distribution it has never seen once the policy improves, the generalization problem of Section 4 in a new modality.

### Rule-based and perception-based checkers

When the prompt states something checkable, a pipeline of perception models can check it, the closest analogue of the verifiable rewards of Section 5.

#### GenEval

**GenEval** (Ghosh, Hajishirzi and Schmidt, NeurIPS 2023; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11513), [code](https://github.com/djghosh13/geneval)) evaluates compositional prompts built from templates over the 80 object classes of the COCO detection dataset, in six categories: a single object, two objects, counting, colours, relative position, and colour attribution (two objects with different specified colours). Each prompt carries machine-readable metadata, for example "two dogs" or "a red car to the left of a bench". To score an image, a Mask2Former instance-segmentation model detects objects above a confidence threshold, a CLIP ViT-L/14 model classifies the colour of each detected object among ten colour names by zero-shot classification on the object's crop, and relative positions are decided from bounding-box centres with a small margin. As a benchmark the result is **strict**: an image counts as correct only if every clause in the metadata holds.

Flow-GRPO uses a softened version as its training reward, through a separate reward server ([code](https://github.com/yifan123/reward-server)). Each clause contributes a partial score: a count clause asking for $n$ objects when $\hat n$ are detected contributes $1 - |n - \hat n|/n$, a colour clause contributes the same expression with $\hat n$ the number of detected objects of the right colour, and a position clause contributes $1$ or $0$. The reward is the average over clauses, while evaluation still uses the strict pass/fail score. The softening matters for GRPO: with a binary reward, a group in which every image fails, which is common for hard counting prompts early in training, has identical rewards and therefore zero advantage, the uninformative-group problem of Section 5. Partial credit gives such groups a ranking to learn from.

#### OCR reward for text rendering

For prompts that ask for a string $s$ to appear in the image, Flow-GRPO reads the image with an OCR model (PaddleOCR in the released code) to get a string $\hat s$. After removing spaces and lower-casing both strings, the reward is
$$
r(x_0, c) = 1 - \frac{\min\big( d(\hat s, s),\, |s| \big)}{|s|},
$$
where $d$ is the Levenshtein edit distance and $|s|$ the length of the target, with $d$ set to $0$ whenever $s$ occurs inside $\hat s$. The reward therefore lies in $[0, 1]$, gives partial credit for near misses, and, because containment counts as a perfect match, does not penalize extra text elsewhere in the image, a gap that a policy could in principle exploit by scattering text.

#### Geometry and physics

For video, geometry foundation models can recover cameras and depth from frames and test whether they are mutually consistent; VGGRPO's motion-smoothness and reprojection rewards, described below, are of this kind.

These rewards are only as good as the perception models inside them. A detector that misses a small object or an OCR model that misreads a stylized font produces a confident wrong reward, and a policy optimized against it can learn to satisfy the checker rather than the prompt, for instance by drawing objects in the exaggerated, isolated way the detector finds easiest.

### Vision-language models as judges

A VLM can be asked directly whether an image matches its prompt. Its advantage over CLIP score is that the prompt is read by a language model, which can follow negation, counting and relations; its costs are speed, the absence of useful gradients in practice, and the perception failures and hallucinations of Section 7.

#### VQAScore

**VQAScore** (Lin et al., ECCV 2024; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01291), [code](https://github.com/linzhiqiu/t2v_metrics)) turns a VLM into a scalar score with a single question. The default template is

> Does this figure show "$c$"? Please answer Yes or No.

and the score is the probability the VLM assigns to the answer "Yes",
$$
r_{\mathrm{VQA}}(x_0, c) = P_{\mathrm{VLM}}\big( \text{Yes} \mid x_0,\ \text{question}(c) \big).
$$
Using the answer probability rather than a generated rating gives a continuous score without prompting the model to invent numbers. The original paper used a CLIP-FlanT5 model trained for this purpose and introduced it alongside GenAI-Bench, a benchmark of compositional prompts; the released library now computes VQAScore with many open and proprietary VLMs, for images and also for video. VQAScore is mainly used for evaluation, as a stronger replacement for CLIP score, but it is equally usable as a non-differentiable RL reward.

#### UnifiedReward

**UnifiedReward** (Wang et al., 2025; [project page](https://codegoat24.github.io/UnifiedReward/), [code](https://github.com/CodeGoat24/UnifiedReward)) is a VLM fine-tuned as a reward model for both multimodal understanding and generation, for images and video. It supports two modes: **pairwise ranking**, judging which of two outputs is better, and **pointwise scoring**, rating a single output. The project has since grown into a family that includes a version producing chain-of-thought reasoning before its judgement (UnifiedReward-Think) and a GRPO variant for text-to-image models trained on pairwise preferences rather than pointwise scores (Pref-GRPO). The Flow-GRPO code supports UnifiedReward as a reward. Pairwise judges fit group methods naturally, since what GRPO needs is a ranking of the $K$ samples for a prompt rather than calibrated absolute scores.

Image-quality assessment models built on VLMs, such as DeQA, are used in the same way for technical quality rather than prompt alignment.

### Video reward models

Video adds properties that no image scorer sees: whether motion is natural, whether objects and identities persist, whether the camera behaves plausibly.

#### VideoReward (VideoAlign)

**VideoReward** (Liu et al., 2025; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13918), [project page](https://gongyeliu.github.io/videoalign/), [code](https://github.com/KwaiVGI/VideoAlign)) is a VLM-based reward model, built on Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct and trained on human preference annotations of generated videos. It scores a video along three dimensions, defined in its release as:

- **Visual quality (VQ)**: clarity, aesthetics and the reasonableness of single frames;
- **Motion quality (MQ)**: dynamic stability, reasonableness and naturalness of motion, and its degree;
- **Text alignment (TA)**: relevance of the video to the prompt.

Separate scores let a training run target one property; DanceGRPO trains HunyuanVideo and SkyReels-I2V on the VQ and MQ outputs, and VGGRPO uses the model's visual- and motion-quality judgements as evaluation metrics. The same paper introduced Flow-DPO, a DPO variant for flow models, and VideoGen-RewardBench, a benchmark for evaluating video reward models themselves, a reminder that reward models need their own evaluation.

#### VBench

**VBench** (Huang et al., CVPR 2024) is an evaluation suite rather than a single reward: it decomposes video quality into many dimensions, such as subject and background consistency, motion smoothness, dynamic degree, aesthetic and imaging quality, each measured by a separate specialist model. It is mostly used for evaluation, and its **dynamic degree** dimension is the usual check on a failure that every motion reward invites: a nearly static video scores well on smoothness and consistency.

### Training on the evaluator

Many of these models are also the standard benchmarks. Flow-GRPO trains on the GenEval checks and the OCR metric and reports them; DanceGRPO trains on HPS-v2.1 and reports it. Such results measure how well the policy optimizes the reward, which is what those experiments are designed to show, but they are weak evidence of a better generator: the policy has been optimized directly against the scorer, and Goodhart's law (Section 4) predicts the score will rise faster than the quality it was meant to track. Careful papers therefore also report rewards they did not train on, check other quality and diversity measures, and run human evaluations; the multi-reward advantages of DanceGRPO and VGGRPO are partly a defence of the same kind, since a sample has to satisfy several imperfect judges at once.

| Reward | What it measures | How it is built | Prompt-aware | Differentiable | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CLIP score | image–text agreement | contrastive embeddings | yes | yes | counting, relations, text |
| Aesthetic predictor | visual appeal | head on CLIP features, human ratings | no | yes | prompt-agnostic collapse |
| ImageReward, PickScore, HPS | human preference | Bradley–Terry on human pairs | yes | yes | annotator bias, saturation |
| GenEval checks, OCR | composition, rendered text | detectors, OCR | yes | no | perception errors |
| VLM judges (VQAScore, UnifiedReward) | prompt alignment, quality | VLM probabilities or fine-tuned VLM | yes | rarely used | cost, hallucination |
| VideoReward / VideoAlign (VQ, MQ, TA) | video quality, motion, alignment | VLM reward model, human pairs | yes | not in practice | static-video bias |
| Geometry rewards | 3D and camera consistency | geometry foundation models | no | yes | checks consistency, not content |

### Reward hacking and regularization

Generators find the failure modes of their reward models with impressive efficiency. Recurring symptoms are **oversaturation** and excessive contrast and detail, which many preference models over-reward; **prompt-agnostic collapse**, where an aesthetic reward drives every prompt towards the same high-scoring style or subject; and **diversity loss**, where samples for a given prompt become near-duplicates because the reward has a single mode.

The defences parallel Section 4: a KL penalty to the reference (per-step as in DPOK, or per-step Gaussian KL in Flow-GRPO), early stopping on a held-out reward, combining several rewards so that no single one can be exploited, interpolating fine-tuned and reference weights, and evaluating with a reward model different from the one optimized. None is fully satisfactory; how to regularize against diversity loss, rather than merely detect it, is an open question.

## Video generation

Video models are diffusion or flow models over a space-time latent, so every method above applies in principle, but:

- **Cost per sample.** One rollout is a full video generation, and group methods need several per prompt. Fewer denoising steps, truncation, LoRA, and low resolution or short clips are standard economies.
- **Rewards.** Per-frame image rewards miss what makes video good or bad: temporal consistency (identities and objects that persist), plausible motion, and physical coherence. Video reward models are less mature than image ones, and a model optimized for per-frame aesthetics can learn to reduce motion, since a static video scores well frame by frame.
- **Long horizons.** Long videos are often generated in chunks, adding a second sequential structure on top of denoising; credit assignment across chunks is largely unsolved.

### Geometry rewards in latent space: VGGRPO

World consistency is a reward that per-frame scores cannot express: a camera should move smoothly, and the same scene seen from two viewpoints should have the same 3D structure. An et al. (2026) target exactly this with **VGGRPO** (Visual Geometry GRPO; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26599), [project page](https://zhaochongan.github.io/projects/VGGRPO)), which runs Flow-GRPO on a pretrained text-to-video flow model with rewards computed by a geometry model. Their starting point is a cost problem. A **geometry foundation model** $\Phi$ maps RGB frames to per-frame camera poses $C_i$, depth maps $D_i$ and point maps $P_i$ in a shared frame (and, for models built for dynamic scenes, scene flow $F_i$). Using it as a reward on a latent video model means decoding every one of the $K$ rollouts per prompt through the VAE before scoring, which dominates the cost of a group update and, the authors argue, makes the reward sensitive to decoding noise.

The fix is a **latent geometry model** built by model stitching. Write the geometry model as a stack of $L$ transformer layers, $\Phi = \Phi_{\ell+1:L} \circ \Phi_{1:\ell}$, and let $\mathcal{E}$ be the video VAE encoder. Replace the first $\hat\ell$ layers by a small 3D-convolutional connector $S_\psi$ that reads latents directly, choosing the cut and the connector together by feature matching on a calibration set of videos $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(M)}$:
$$
\hat\ell, \psi = \arg\min_{\ell, \psi} \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \big\| S_\psi(\mathcal{E}(x^{(m)})) - \Phi_{1:\ell}(x^{(m)}) \big\|_2^2 ,
\qquad \hat\Phi_\psi = \Phi_{\hat\ell+1:L} \circ S_\psi .
$$
The connector and the remaining layers are then fine-tuned so that the outputs of $\hat\Phi_\psi(\mathcal{E}(x))$ match those of $\Phi(x)$, with a weighted $\ell_1$ loss over the geometry outputs. After this, geometry is read off a generated latent $z_0$ as $\hat\Phi_\psi(z_0)$, with no decoding.

Two rewards are defined on that geometry.

- **Camera motion smoothness.** From the predicted camera centres $c_i$, form velocities $v_i = c_{i+1} - c_i$ and accelerations $a_i = v_i - v_{i-1}$, and measure the scale-normalized acceleration
$$
e_{\mathrm{trans}}(z_0) = \frac{1}{N - 2} \sum_{i=2}^{N-1} \frac{\|a_i\|_2}{\|v_i\|_2 + \|v_{i-1}\|_2}
$$
over the $N$ frames. A rotational error $e_{\mathrm{rot}}$ is defined the same way from angular velocities $\omega_i = \log_{SO(3)}(R_i^\top R_{i+1})$. Both are near zero for steady motion, and the reward $r_{\mathrm{motion}} = \tfrac12 \big( (1 + e_{\mathrm{trans}})^{-1} + (1 + e_{\mathrm{rot}})^{-1} \big)$ lies in $(0, 1]$.
- **Geometry reprojection consistency.** Aggregate the predicted point maps into one scene point cloud (for dynamic scenes, keeping only points the predicted scene flow marks as static), project it into each view $i$ with the predicted camera to get a rendered depth $\hat D_i$, and compare with the predicted depth: $e^{(i)}_{\mathrm{geo}}$ is the mean of $|\hat D_i - D_i|$ over the validly projected pixels. The reward $r_{\mathrm{geo}}$ is minus the average of $e^{(i)}_{\mathrm{geo}}$ over the three worst views, so that it targets local failures rather than averaging them away.

The two rewards live on different scales, so, as in DanceGRPO, they are normalized **separately** within the group before being combined:
$$
\hat A^{(k)} = \frac12 \left( \frac{r_{\mathrm{motion}}^{(k)} - \mu_{\mathrm{motion}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{motion}}} + \frac{r_{\mathrm{geo}}^{(k)} - \mu_{\mathrm{geo}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{geo}}} \right),
$$
where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of each reward across the $K$ samples for the prompt. Normalizing a weighted sum instead would let whichever reward has the larger spread within a group decide the ranking; normalizing each first gives the two equal say in every group, whatever their units. The policy update is then the clipped Flow-GRPO surrogate of the previous subsection, with per-step ratios over denoising latents and a KL penalty to the reference.

<Remark>
VGGRPO illustrates three points that recur in RL for generators. First, the **reward model is a design object**: here it is a perception model (Section 7's territory) stitched onto the generator's latent space, and reward cost, not policy cost, is what it optimizes. Second, a reward on one property shifts others: the authors report a slightly lower dynamic-degree score after training, which they attribute to reduced camera jitter lowering the optical-flow magnitude that metric measures. Any reward that touches motion calls for checking that the video has not simply become more static. Third, because $\hat\Phi_\psi$ is differentiable, the same rewards can also be used without RL, as gradient guidance applied to latents during sampling. The authors report that this helps less than the RL fine-tuning, the same contrast between pathwise and policy-gradient uses of a reward drawn earlier in this section.
</Remark>

## Autoregressive image models

Autoregressive models tokenize images with a discrete tokenizer and generate tokens one at a time. For these models the policy is literally the token policy of Section 4: $\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ is a sum of token log-probabilities, and PPO, GRPO and DPO apply without any of the reformulations of this section.

## Open problems

- **Credit assignment over denoising steps.** Early steps fix layout and later ones detail, yet all receive the same terminal reward; intermediate rewards or value functions over noisy states are possible remedies, none standard.
- **Where to inject noise.** The SDE noise exists only to enable policy gradients; which steps should be stochastic, and how much, is an active design question.
- **Reward models.** Robust, hard-to-hack rewards for composition, text, motion and physical plausibility.
- **Few-step and distilled generators.** Whether to apply RL before or after distillation to few-step samplers, and how, is unsettled.

Section 9 turns to robot policies. Many current vision-language-action models produce their actions with a diffusion or flow-matching head, and such a head is a generator of exactly the kind studied here, whose samples are action chunks instead of images. The denoising MDP, the ODE-to-SDE conversion and Flow-GRPO's per-step Gaussian log-probabilities carry over directly; what is new is that each generated chunk is executed by a robot, real or simulated, so the denoising chain becomes the inner loop of a longer decision process with its own dynamics and rewards.
