Section 1 solved the Bellman equations with the model in hand. An agent acting in the world does not have the model. It has a stream of transitions $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$, one at a time, produced by its own behaviour. This section is about turning that stream into value estimates. The central idea, **temporal-difference (TD) learning**, treats each transition as a noisy sample of one Bellman backup and moves the current estimate a small step towards it. Because the target is built from the estimate itself, TD methods **bootstrap**: they learn a guess from a guess. That makes them fast and online, and it is also the source of most of the instabilities of value-based RL.

Policy gradients dominate the rest of these notes, but value learning keeps reappearing inside them: the value head trained alongside PPO (Sections 3 and 4), the critics of off-policy robot RL and the pitfalls of offline RL (Section 7), process reward models (Section 5), and a per-step form of KL-regularized RL in which a language model's log-probabilities are soft Q-values. This section develops the tabular theory carefully and then follows it into function approximation, deep networks and continuous actions.

## The prediction problem

The **prediction** problem is to estimate $V^\pi$ for a fixed policy $\pi$ from transitions generated by following it; the **control** problem, later, is to find a good policy. Throughout, $\alpha$ is a step size and the MDP is finite with bounded rewards unless stated otherwise.

### TD(0)

The Bellman expectation equation of Section 1 is a statement about a single transition:
$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[ r_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) \,\big|\, s_t = s \big] = (T^\pi V^\pi)(s).
$$
Each observed transition is a sample of the quantity inside the expectation, except for the unknown $V^\pi(s_{t+1})$, for which TD learning substitutes the current estimate.

<Definition name="TD(0)">
Given a table $V$ and a transition $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ with $a_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s_t)$, the **TD(0)** update is
$$
V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha_t\, \delta_t, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t).
$$
The quantity $r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1})$ is the **TD target** and $\delta_t$ the **TD error**. At a transition into a terminal state, $V(s_{t+1})$ is taken to be $0$.
</Definition>

### TD as stochastic approximation

**Stochastic approximation** seeks a zero of $h(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[H(\theta, \xi)]$ from noisy samples, by the iteration $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + \alpha_k H(\theta_k, \xi_k)$. Under suitable stability conditions it converges when the step sizes satisfy the **Robbins–Monro conditions**
$$
\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty, \qquad \sum_k \alpha_k^2 \lt \infty .
$$
The first lets the iterates forget the initial error; the second makes the accumulated noise summable. A typical choice is $\alpha_k = 1/k$. To see TD(0) in this form, add and subtract the expected target:
$$
V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha_t \Big[ (T^\pi V)(s_t) - V(s_t) + \eta_t \Big], \qquad \eta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - (T^\pi V)(s_t).
$$
Given the past and $s_t$, the noise $\eta_t$ has mean zero, because $a_t$, $r_t$ and $s_{t+1}$ are drawn exactly as in the definition of $T^\pi$. So TD(0) is a noisy version of the damped iteration $V \leftarrow (1 - \alpha) V + \alpha\, T^\pi V$, a sup-norm contraction with fixed point $V^\pi$, applied to one coordinate (the visited state) at a time.

<Result name="Convergence of tabular TD(0)">
In a finite MDP with $\gamma \lt 1$, suppose every state is visited infinitely often and the step sizes used at each state satisfy the Robbins–Monro conditions. Then TD(0) converges to $V^\pi$ with probability one.
</Result>

The noise has mean zero and conditional variance at most $C(1 + \|V\|_\infty^2)$, and general theorems on asynchronous stochastic approximation of sup-norm contractions with such noise, due to Jaakkola, Jordan and Singh (1994) and Tsitsiklis (1994), give the result; they also cover Q-learning below, and episodic tasks with $\gamma = 1$ in which every policy terminates. TD learning was introduced by Sutton (1988). In practice the step size is usually constant, so the estimate keeps fluctuating around $V^\pi$ with a spread that shrinks with $\alpha$ — acceptable, and even desirable when the environment or policy keeps changing.

## Monte Carlo versus temporal differences

The **Monte Carlo** alternative moves $V(s_t)$ towards the observed return, $V(s_t) \leftarrow V(s_t) + \alpha (G_t - V(s_t))$, and converges to $V^\pi$ under the same conditions. The two differ in four ways.

- **Bias.** $G_t$ is an unbiased sample of $V^\pi(s_t)$; the TD target is biased whenever $V \ne V^\pi$, although its fixed point is correct.
- **Variance.** $G_t$ depends on every reward and transition until the episode ends; the TD target on one of each, so its variance is typically much smaller.
- **Timing.** TD updates after every step and works in continuing tasks; Monte Carlo waits for the episode to end.
- **The Markov property.** TD relies on $s_{t+1}$ summarizing the future; Monte Carlo does not, which makes it more robust when the "state" is only a partial observation.

### The batch view

The comparison sharpens on a fixed batch of episodes, replayed with a small constant step size, summing the updates over each sweep, until the estimates stop changing. **Batch Monte Carlo** converges to the average return observed after each state, the least-squares fit $\min_V \sum_t (G_t - V(s_t))^2$. **Batch TD(0)** converges somewhere else. At a fixed point the summed update for each state $s$ is zero:
$$
\sum_{t \,:\, s_t = s} \big( r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s) \big) = 0 .
$$
Divide by the number of visits $n(s)$, and write $\hat r(s)$ for the average reward observed on leaving $s$ and $\hat P(s' \mid s)$ for the fraction of visits to $s$ followed by $s'$. The condition becomes
$$
V(s) = \hat r(s) + \gamma \sum_{s'} \hat P(s' \mid s)\, V(s'),
$$
the Bellman equation of the empirical model. Since $\hat P$ and $\hat r$ are maximum-likelihood estimates, batch TD(0) computes the **certainty-equivalence estimate**: the exact value of the policy in the maximum-likelihood model, treated as if it were the truth. (For $\gamma \lt 1$ or episodic data, $I - \gamma \hat P$ is invertible and the batch iteration converges for small enough $\alpha$.) TD is implicitly model-based.

<Example>
Take two states $A$ and $B$, no discounting, and a batch of eight episodes. In one, the agent starts in $A$, moves to $B$ with reward $0$, and then terminates with reward $0$. In six, it starts in $B$ and terminates with reward $1$. In the last, it starts in $B$ and terminates with reward $0$. State $B$ was visited eight times, with return $1$ six times, so both methods give $V(B) = 3/4$.

They disagree about $A$. Its only visit was followed by return $0$, so batch Monte Carlo sets $V(A) = 0$, which fits the observed data perfectly. Batch TD reasons through the model instead: in the data, $A$ always leads to $B$ with reward $0$, so $V(A) = 0 + V(B) = 3/4$. If the process really is Markov, the TD answer is the better prediction, because it pools all eight observations of what follows $B$. (The example is from Sutton and Barto's textbook.)
</Example>

<Remark>
In a **tree-structured** MDP, where every state has a unique history — text generation, with $s_t = (x, y_{\lt t})$, is the prime example — paths never merge, so the empirical model has $\hat P(s' \mid s) = n(s')/n(s)$ for each child $s'$. Solving the empirical Bellman equation from the leaves upwards gives exactly the average return of the episodes through each state, so batch TD and batch Monte Carlo coincide. For language models any advantage of bootstrapping must come from a function approximator generalizing across similar prefixes, not from pooling.
</Remark>

## Multi-step returns and TD($\lambda$)

Between one-step TD and Monte Carlo lies a family of targets. The **$n$-step return**
$$
G_t^{(n)} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^k r_{t+k} + \gamma^n V(s_{t+n})
$$
uses $n$ real rewards and then bootstraps; if the episode ends before $t + n$, it is just $G_t$. So $n = 1$ gives the TD target and $n = \infty$ the Monte Carlo return. Since $\mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t^{(n)} \mid s_t = s] = ((T^\pi)^n V)(s)$, the bias inherited from a wrong $V$ shrinks like $\gamma^n$ in the sup norm, while the variance grows with the number of sampled rewards.

### The forward view

Rather than choose one $n$, average all of them with geometric weights. The **$\lambda$-return**, for $\lambda \in [0, 1)$, is
$$
G_t^\lambda = (1 - \lambda) \sum_{n \ge 1} \lambda^{n-1} G_t^{(n)} .
$$
The weights sum to one. In an episode ending at time $T$, every $G_t^{(n)}$ with $n \ge T - t$ equals $G_t$, so the tail weight $\lambda^{T-t-1}$ falls on the full return, and $\lambda \to 1$ recovers Monte Carlo. The **forward view** of TD($\lambda$) updates $V(s_t)$ towards $G_t^\lambda$.

Splitting off the first reward in each $G_t^{(n)}$ gives $G_t^\lambda = r_t + \gamma [ (1 - \lambda) V(s_{t+1}) + \lambda\, G_{t+1}^\lambda ]$; subtracting $V(s_t)$ and unrolling,
$$
G_t^\lambda - V(s_t) = \delta_t + \gamma \lambda \big( G_{t+1}^\lambda - V(s_{t+1}) \big) = \sum_{k \ge 0} (\gamma \lambda)^k \delta_{t+k},
$$
with $V$ held fixed. The $\lambda$-return error is a discounted sum of future TD errors. This is precisely generalized advantage estimation (Section 3), where the same sum is used as an advantage and $G_t^\lambda$ as the critic's regression target.

### The backward view

The forward view needs future rewards. The **backward view** sends each TD error back to the states that led to it, online. Keep an **eligibility trace** $e_t(s)$ for every state, with $e_{-1} = 0$ and
$$
e_t(s) = \gamma \lambda\, e_{t-1}(s) + \mathbf{1}[s_t = s],
$$
and after each step update every state: $V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\, \delta_t\, e_t(s)$. Recently and frequently visited states receive the most credit for the current surprise.

<Result name="Equivalence of the forward and backward views">
Suppose $V$ is held fixed during an episode and the updates are summed and applied at its end. Then for every state $s$ the total backward-view update equals the total forward-view update:
$$
\sum_t \alpha\, \delta_t\, e_t(s) = \sum_t \alpha \big( G_t^\lambda - V(s_t) \big) \mathbf{1}[s_t = s].
$$
</Result>

*Proof.* Substitute the sum of TD errors for $G_t^\lambda - V(s_t)$ and exchange the order of summation:
$$
\sum_t \mathbf{1}[s_t = s] \sum_{k \ge t} (\gamma \lambda)^{k - t} \delta_k = \sum_k \delta_k \sum_{t \le k} (\gamma \lambda)^{k - t}\, \mathbf{1}[s_t = s] = \sum_k \delta_k\, e_k(s),
$$
since unrolling the trace recursion gives $e_k(s) = \sum_{t \le k} (\gamma \lambda)^{k-t} \mathbf{1}[s_t = s]$. $\square$

Online, the two views differ slightly because $V$ changes within the episode; modified traces restore exact equivalence. Tabular TD($\lambda$) converges under the same conditions as TD(0). With function approximation the trace accumulates gradients, $e_t = \gamma \lambda\, e_{t-1} + \nabla_w V_w(s_t)$. Intermediate $\lambda$ usually works best, but the choice is empirical.

## Control: SARSA, Expected SARSA and Q-learning

Without a model one learns action values, since acting greedily with respect to $Q$ needs no knowledge of $P$. **Generalized policy iteration** interleaves partial evaluation of the current policy with improvement towards the greedy one. Three TD methods differ only in the target.

- **SARSA** uses the action actually taken next, $a_{t+1} \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s_{t+1})$:
$$
Q(s_t, a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t, a_t) + \alpha \big( r_t + \gamma Q(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1}) - Q(s_t, a_t) \big).
$$
- **Expected SARSA** replaces $Q(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1})$ by its average $\sum_{a'} \pi(a' \mid s_{t+1})\, Q(s_{t+1}, a')$ under a target policy $\pi$, removing the variance from sampling $a_{t+1}$.
- **Q-learning** uses the greedy target, which is Expected SARSA with $\pi$ greedy with respect to $Q$:
$$
Q(s_t, a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t, a_t) + \alpha \big( r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a') - Q(s_t, a_t) \big).
$$

### Q-learning as sampled value iteration

Define the Bellman optimality operator on action-value functions,
$$
(T^\ast Q)(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \max_{a'} Q(s', a').
$$
Its fixed point is $Q^\ast$, and the argument of Section 1 — now with $|\max_{a'} Q_1(s', a') - \max_{a'} Q_2(s', a')| \le \|Q_1 - Q_2\|_\infty$ inside an average over $s'$ — shows it is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm, so value iteration on $Q$ converges geometrically to $Q^\ast$. Now look at a single transition. The target $y_t = r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a')$ satisfies
$$
\mathbb{E}\big[ y_t \mid s_t, a_t \big] = (T^\ast Q)(s_t, a_t),
$$
where the expectation is over the reward and $s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t)$ only. How $a_t$ was chosen does not enter. Q-learning, introduced by Watkins (1989), is therefore sampled value iteration, one state–action pair at a time, and it is **off-policy**: the data may come from any behaviour policy — old policies, demonstrations, a random one.

<Result name="Convergence of tabular Q-learning">
In a finite MDP with $\gamma \lt 1$, suppose every state–action pair is updated infinitely often and the step sizes used at each pair satisfy the Robbins–Monro conditions. Then $Q_t \to Q^\ast$ with probability one, whatever the behaviour policy.
</Result>

Watkins and Dayan (1992) gave the first proof. The stochastic-approximation argument is that of TD(0): the update is $Q \leftarrow Q + \alpha [ T^\ast Q - Q + \eta ]$ at the visited pair, with $\eta = y_t - (T^\ast Q)(s_t, a_t)$ of mean zero and conditional variance at most $C(1 + \|Q\|_\infty^2)$, and $T^\ast$ is a sup-norm contraction. Once $Q_t$ is within half the smallest nonzero action gap of $Q^\ast$, the greedy policy is optimal. The theorem says nothing about rates, which depend on how the behaviour policy explores.

SARSA, by contrast, is **on-policy**: it estimates $Q^\pi$ for the policy it follows, so to reach $Q^\ast$ the policy must become greedy while still exploring. A sequence of policies is **greedy in the limit with infinite exploration (GLIE)** if every state–action pair is visited infinitely often and the policies converge to the greedy policy with respect to the current $Q$. Singh et al. (2000) showed that tabular SARSA with a GLIE policy and Robbins–Monro step sizes converges to $Q^\ast$.

### Exploration

The convergence theorems assume exploration; they do not provide it.

- **$\varepsilon$-greedy** takes a uniformly random action with probability $\varepsilon$, otherwise the greedy one. Decaying $\varepsilon$ at each state like the reciprocal of its visit count gives a GLIE schedule. Being undirected, it can be very slow: in a chain where only one action makes progress, reaching the far end takes a number of steps exponential in the chain's length.
- **Boltzmann (softmax)** exploration samples $a$ with probability proportional to $\exp(Q(s, a)/\beta)$, so clearly bad actions are rarely tried; the same form reappears below as the optimal policy of entropy-regularized RL.
- **Optimism** initializes $Q$ high, say at $R_{\max}/(1 - \gamma)$, or adds a bonus that shrinks with the visit count, so a greedy agent is drawn to untried actions until experience pulls their estimates down. Bonus methods have sample-complexity guarantees in the tabular case; deep RL uses pseudo-counts or prediction errors as bonuses.

```
Q-learning with epsilon-greedy exploration
initialize Q(s, a) arbitrarily, with Q(terminal, .) = 0
for each episode:
    s = initial state
    while s is not terminal:
        with probability epsilon: a = random action
        otherwise:                a = argmax over b of Q(s, b)
        take a, observe reward r and next state s2
        target = r                                if s2 is terminal
                 r + gamma * max over b of Q(s2, b)   otherwise
        Q(s, a) = Q(s, a) + alpha * (target - Q(s, a))
        s = s2
```

## Maximization bias and double Q-learning

Q-learning's target takes a maximum of noisy estimates, and that maximum is biased upwards.

<Result name="Maximization bias">
For random variables $X_1, \dots, X_m$ with finite means,
$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[ \max_i X_i \Big] \ge \max_i \mathbb{E}[X_i].
$$
</Result>

*Proof.* The maximum is a convex function, so this is Jensen's inequality. More directly, $\max_i X_i \ge X_j$ for each $j$; take expectations, then the maximum over $j$. $\square$

The inequality is usually strict: if the true action values at $s'$ are all $0$ and the estimates are independent and unbiased, $\max_a \hat Q(s', a)$ has positive mean — for two independent $N(0, \sigma^2)$ estimates, $\mathbb{E}[\max(X, Y)] = \tfrac12 \mathbb{E}|X - Y| = \sigma/\sqrt{\pi}$. Bootstrapping propagates the overestimate backwards, and states with many actions and noisy rewards look spuriously attractive.

The source of the bias is that the same noisy estimates are used both to **select** the maximizing action and to **evaluate** it. **Double Q-learning** (van Hasselt, 2010) decouples the two. It keeps two tables $Q^A$ and $Q^B$; at each step one of them, chosen at random, is updated, say $Q^A$, with target
$$
r_t + \gamma\, Q^B\big( s_{t+1}, a^\ast \big), \qquad a^\ast = \arg\max_{a'} Q^A(s_{t+1}, a'),
$$
and symmetrically for $Q^B$. Behaviour can use $Q^A + Q^B$. To see why the upward bias disappears, condition on $Q^A$, which fixes $a^\ast$. If $Q^B(s_{t+1}, \cdot)$ is an unbiased estimate of the true values $q(\cdot)$ and independent of $Q^A$, then
$$
\mathbb{E}\big[ Q^B(s_{t+1}, a^\ast) \,\big|\, Q^A \big] = q(a^\ast) \le \max_{a'} q(a').
$$
The double estimator can underestimate, when $a^\ast$ is not the best action, but it has no upward bias. The same decoupling reappears as Double DQN and in the clipped double-Q targets of TD3 and SAC.

## Linear function approximation

Tables do not generalize. With features $\phi : \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}^k$ and a linear estimate $V_w(s) = \phi(s)^\top w$, the TD(0) update becomes
$$
w \leftarrow w + \alpha\, \delta_t\, \phi(s_t), \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma \phi(s_{t+1})^\top w - \phi(s_t)^\top w .
$$
This is a **semi-gradient** method: the gradient of $\tfrac12 (\text{target} - V_w(s_t))^2$ with the target treated as a constant, although it depends on $w$.

### The TD fixed point

Assume the policy induces an ergodic Markov chain $P_\pi$ with stationary distribution $d$, $d(s) \gt 0$ for all $s$, and that states are sampled from $d$ — the **on-policy** case. Let $D = \mathrm{diag}(d)$, let $\Phi$ be the $|\mathcal{S}| \times k$ matrix with rows $\phi(s)^\top$, of full column rank, and write $\|V\|_D^2 = \sum_s d(s) V(s)^2$. The $D$-orthogonal projection onto the span of the features is $\Pi = \Phi (\Phi^\top D \Phi)^{-1} \Phi^\top D$.

The expected update is
$$
\mathbb{E}\big[ \delta_t\, \phi(s_t) \big] = \Phi^\top D \big( r_\pi + \gamma P_\pi \Phi w - \Phi w \big) = b - A w, \qquad A = \Phi^\top D (I - \gamma P_\pi) \Phi, \quad b = \Phi^\top D r_\pi .
$$
Since $A$ is generally not symmetric, $b - Aw$ is not the gradient of any objective. A fixed point satisfies $\Phi^\top D (T^\pi \Phi w - \Phi w) = 0$: the Bellman residual is $D$-orthogonal to the span of the features. Equivalently,
$$
\Phi w = \Pi\, T^\pi\, \Phi w .
$$
This is the **projected Bellman equation**; its solution $w_{\mathrm{TD}}$ is the **TD fixed point**. TD finds not the best approximation $\Pi V^\pi$ but the fixed point of "apply the Bellman operator, then project".

Two facts about the on-policy weighting make this well behaved.

1. **$P_\pi$ is non-expansive in $\|\cdot\|_D$.** Jensen's inequality, $(\sum_{s'} P_\pi(s, s') V(s'))^2 \le \sum_{s'} P_\pi(s, s') V(s')^2$, averaged over $s \sim d$ and combined with stationarity $d^\top P_\pi = d^\top$, gives $\|P_\pi V\|_D \le \|V\|_D$. Hence $T^\pi$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in $\|\cdot\|_D$, and so is $\Pi T^\pi$, because an orthogonal projection is non-expansive. The projected equation has a unique solution.
2. **$A$ is positive definite.** For $u \ne 0$ and $v = \Phi u$, Cauchy–Schwarz and fact 1 give $u^\top A u = \|v\|_D^2 - \gamma \langle v, P_\pi v \rangle_D \ge (1 - \gamma) \|v\|_D^2 \gt 0$, so the mean dynamics $\dot w = b - A w$ are globally stable.

<Result name="Linear TD (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, 1997)">
Under the assumptions above, with Robbins–Monro step sizes, linear TD(0) converges with probability one to the TD fixed point $w_{\mathrm{TD}}$; the same holds for linear TD($\lambda$) with its own projected fixed point. Moreover
$$
\big\| \Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - V^\pi \big\|_D \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \gamma^2}}\, \big\| \Pi V^\pi - V^\pi \big\|_D .
$$
</Result>

*Proof of the bound.* Write $\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - V^\pi = (\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - \Pi V^\pi) + (\Pi V^\pi - V^\pi)$. The first term lies in the span of the features and the second is $D$-orthogonal to it, so by Pythagoras
$$
\|\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - V^\pi\|_D^2 = \|\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - \Pi V^\pi\|_D^2 + \|\Pi V^\pi - V^\pi\|_D^2 .
$$
Using $\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} = \Pi T^\pi \Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}}$ and $V^\pi = T^\pi V^\pi$, together with fact 1,
$$
\|\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - \Pi V^\pi\|_D = \|\Pi T^\pi \Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - \Pi T^\pi V^\pi\|_D \le \gamma \|\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - V^\pi\|_D .
$$
Substituting, $(1 - \gamma^2) \|\Phi w_{\mathrm{TD}} - V^\pi\|_D^2 \le \|\Pi V^\pi - V^\pi\|_D^2$. $\square$

The right-hand side is the error of the best approximation in the feature space, which is where linear Monte Carlo regression converges. TD can lose a factor $1/\sqrt{1 - \gamma^2}$, large when $\gamma$ is near $1$, in exchange for lower variance and online learning.

### The deadly triad

The proof used the on-policy weighting twice: stationarity of $d$ gave fact 1, and fact 1 gave both the contraction and the positive definiteness of $A$. Under an off-policy state distribution neither need hold.

<Example>
Take two states with a single weight $w$ and features $1$ and $2$, so $V_w(s_1) = w$ and $V_w(s_2) = 2w$. Suppose the data contain only the transition $s_1 \to s_2$ with reward $0$, as can happen when a behaviour policy differs from the target policy. Then $\delta = 0 + \gamma \cdot 2w - w = (2\gamma - 1) w$, and the update $w \leftarrow w + \alpha (2\gamma - 1) w$ multiplies $w$ by $1 + \alpha(2\gamma - 1)$. For $\gamma \gt 1/2$ and any $w \ne 0$, $w$ diverges. Raising $V_w(s_1)$ raises its own target twice as fast, and the transition out of $s_2$ that would correct this is never sampled.
</Example>

<Remark>
**The deadly triad**, in Sutton and Barto's phrase, is the combination of **function approximation** that generalizes across states, **bootstrapping** (targets built from current estimates) and **off-policy training** (updates under a distribution other than the target policy's). When all three are present, value estimates can diverge, even for linear function approximation, a fixed target policy and exact expected updates; Baird's counterexample is the classical demonstration. Removing any one ingredient restores stability in the linear case: tabular methods are safe, Monte Carlo targets reduce the problem to regression, and on-policy linear TD converges by the result above. The triad describes a risk, not a certainty — many practical systems combine all three and work — and with nonlinear function approximation even on-policy TD lacks general guarantees. **Gradient-TD** methods restore linear off-policy convergence by descending the projected Bellman error $\|\Pi T^\pi V_w - V_w\|_D^2$ itself.
</Remark>

## Deep Q-learning

The best-known demonstration that deep networks can nevertheless be trained this way is **DQN** (Mnih et al., 2015), which learned to play many Atari games from raw pixels with a single architecture and set of hyperparameters. It is Q-learning with a convolutional network $Q_w$ and two stabilizing devices. An **experience replay** buffer stores past transitions and trains on random minibatches, breaking correlations and reusing data. A **target network** with parameters $w^-$, a copy of $w$ refreshed only periodically, supplies the bootstrap target, so the loss
$$
L(w) = \mathbb{E}_{(s, a, r, s') \sim \text{buffer}} \Big[ \big( r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_{w^-}(s', a') - Q_w(s, a) \big)^2 \Big]
$$
is a regression onto targets that hold still for many updates (often with a Huber loss in place of the square). Between refreshes this is approximately **fitted Q-iteration**: one step $Q_{k+1} \approx T^\ast Q_k$ of value iteration, solved as a supervised problem. All three ingredients of the deadly triad are present; replay and the target network make instability manageable, not impossible. Standard improvements, each repairing one weakness:

- **Double DQN** (van Hasselt, Guez and Silver, 2016) applies double Q-learning with the networks already at hand: the online network selects, the target network evaluates, $r + \gamma Q_{w^-}(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_w(s', a'))$.
- **Prioritized experience replay** (Schaul et al., 2016) replays transitions with large $|\delta|$ more often, with importance weights correcting the bias.
- **Dueling networks** (Wang et al., 2016) parametrize $Q(s, a) = V(s) + A(s, a) - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|} \sum_{a'} A(s, a')$.
- **Distributional RL** (Bellemare, Dabney and Munos, 2017) learns the whole distribution of the return $Z(s, a)$, via the distributional Bellman equation $Z(s, a) \overset{d}{=} r + \gamma Z(s', a')$.
- **$n$-step targets** use $\sum_{k \lt n} \gamma^k r_{t+k} + \gamma^n \max_{a'} Q_{w^-}(s_{t+n}, a')$. They are not corrected for the replayed actions being off-policy; for small $n$ that bias is tolerated for faster propagation of reward.
- **Rainbow** (Hessel et al., 2018) combined these five with noisy networks, which explore through learned noise in the weights instead of $\varepsilon$-greedy, and measured each component's contribution by ablation.

## Continuous actions and soft Q-learning

Q-learning needs $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$ for every target and action choice; for $\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ that is an inner optimization problem at every step. The standard answer is an **actor–critic** in which a learned actor does the maximization.

**DDPG** (Lillicrap et al., 2016) trains a critic $Q_w$ with the DQN-style target $r + \gamma Q_{w^-}(s', \pi_{\theta^-}(s'))$ and a deterministic actor $a = \pi_\theta(s)$ that follows the **deterministic policy gradient**,
$$
\nabla_\theta\, Q_w(s, \pi_\theta(s)) = \nabla_a Q_w(s, a) \big|_{a = \pi_\theta(s)}\, \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(s),
$$
averaged over replayed states; exploration comes from action noise. The actor exploits any critic error, so overestimation is especially damaging. **TD3** (Fujimoto, van Hoof and Meger, 2018) uses two critics and the **clipped double-Q** target $r + \gamma \min_{i = 1, 2} Q_{w_i^-}(s', \tilde a')$, with smoothing noise on the target action $\tilde a'$ and less frequent actor updates.

### The soft Bellman equation

A third route changes the objective so that the maximization becomes a smooth one. Add a per-step KL penalty towards a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, with coefficient $\beta \gt 0$:
$$
J_\beta(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi \Big[ \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \Big( r(s_t, a_t) - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big( \pi(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \big) \Big) \Big].
$$

<Result name="Soft Bellman optimality equations">
For $\gamma \lt 1$ and bounded rewards, the optimal soft action values $Q_\beta^\ast$ and state values $V_\beta^\ast$ are the unique solution of
$$
Q(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{s' \sim P(\cdot \mid s, a)}\big[ V(s') \big], \qquad V(s) = \beta \log \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid s)} \Big[ \exp\big( Q(s, a)/\beta \big) \Big],
$$
and the optimal policy is
$$
\pi^\ast(a \mid s) = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a \mid s) \exp\Big( \frac{Q_\beta^\ast(s, a) - V_\beta^\ast(s)}{\beta} \Big) \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a \mid s) \exp\big( Q_\beta^\ast(s, a)/\beta \big).
$$
</Result>

The derivation is one step of dynamic programming per state: given $Q(s, \cdot)$, the policy at $s$ should maximize $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[Q(s, a)] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid s))$. By the Gibbs variational principle of Section 3 the maximum is $\beta \log \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[e^{Q(s, \cdot)/\beta}]$, attained by $\pi \propto \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\, e^{Q(s, \cdot)/\beta}$. The "soft maximum" $f \mapsto \beta \log \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[e^{f/\beta}]$ is monotone and commutes with adding a constant, hence is non-expansive in the sup norm, so the soft Bellman operator is a $\gamma$-contraction exactly as $T^\ast$ was, and **soft Q-learning** — Q-learning with the soft maximum in the target — inherits the tabular theory. As $\beta \to 0$ the soft maximum tends to the hard maximum over the support of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$; if $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is uniform, the KL penalty is $\log |\mathcal{A}|$ minus the entropy, and $J_\beta$ becomes the **maximum-entropy** objective with temperature $\beta$, up to a constant.

**Soft actor–critic (SAC)** (Haarnoja et al., 2018) is the continuous-action algorithm built on the entropy-bonus version. In its common form the critics regress onto $r + \gamma \big( \min_i Q_{w_i^-}(s', a') - \beta \log \pi_\theta(a' \mid s') \big)$ with $a' \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s')$, a one-sample soft value of the current policy with TD3's clipped minimum, and the stochastic actor maximizes $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[Q_w(s, a) - \beta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)]$ by reparametrized sampling, moving towards $\exp(Q_w(s, \cdot)/\beta)$. The temperature is often tuned automatically towards a target entropy.

### The token-level view

For a language model the per-step equations recover the sequence-level result of Section 3. Take the token MDP of Section 1: deterministic transitions $s_{t+1} = (s_t, y_t)$, $\gamma = 1$, and reward zero except $r(x, y)$ at the final token $T$. The soft Bellman equations become
$$
Q_\beta^\ast(s_t, y_t) = V_\beta^\ast(s_{t+1}) \quad (t \lt T), \qquad Q_\beta^\ast(s_T, y_T) = r(x, y), \qquad V_\beta^\ast(s_t) = \beta \log \sum_{y_t} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_t \mid s_t)\, e^{Q_\beta^\ast(s_t, y_t)/\beta}.
$$
Unrolling from the end, $e^{V_\beta^\ast(s_t)/\beta}$ is the $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$-expectation of $e^{r(x, y)/\beta}$ over completions of $s_t$; in particular $V_\beta^\ast(s_1) = \beta \log Z(x)$. Multiplying the per-token optimal policies, the exponents telescope:
$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} \big( Q_\beta^\ast(s_t, y_t) - V_\beta^\ast(s_t) \big) = r(x, y) - V_\beta^\ast(s_1), \qquad \text{so} \qquad \pi^\ast(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x, y)/\beta},
$$
which is the closed form of Section 3 behind the KL-regularized RLHF objective and DPO (Section 4). The per-token identity is also worth reading on its own:
$$
\beta \log \frac{\pi^\ast(y_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_t \mid s_t)} = Q_\beta^\ast(s_t, y_t) - V_\beta^\ast(s_t).
$$
The log-ratio of the KL-optimal language model to its reference is, token by token, a soft advantage: a KL-regularized policy implicitly carries a soft Q-function, the starting point for token-level views of preference optimization.

## Offline RL

In **offline RL** the agent learns from a fixed dataset collected by some behaviour policy, with no further interaction. Off-policy Q-learning looks ideally suited but fails in a characteristic way. The target $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$ queries actions that may never appear with $s'$ in the data; there the network's values are pure extrapolation, and the maximum selects exactly the ones that are erroneously high. Online, acting on an overestimate produces data that corrects it; offline nothing does, and bootstrapping compounds the error. Fujimoto et al. (2019) called this **extrapolation error**.

The remedies keep the policy, or the values it relies on, close to the data. One family constrains the policy to the behaviour policy's support. Another is **pessimistic**: **conservative Q-learning** (CQL; Kumar et al., 2020) adds a regularizer that pushes $Q$ down on actions outside the data and up on dataset actions. **Implicit Q-learning** (IQL; Kostrikov, Nair and Levine, 2022) avoids querying unseen actions altogether. It fits $V_\psi(s)$ by **expectile regression** on $Q(s, a)$ over dataset actions, with the asymmetric loss $|\kappa - \mathbf{1}[u \lt 0]|\, u^2$ for $u = Q(s, a) - V_\psi(s)$; for $\kappa$ close to $1$ this approximates a maximum over the actions present in the data. It then trains $Q$ by TD towards $r + \gamma V_\psi(s')$ and extracts a policy by advantage-weighted regression — the same weighted imitation that Section 7 derives from the KL-regularized objective.

## Where value learning reappears

- **Value heads in PPO** (Sections 3 and 4). The critic $V_\psi$ is regressed onto $\lambda$-returns and advantages are discounted sums of TD errors: on-policy TD($\lambda$) with a neural network. With $\gamma = \lambda = 1$ and a terminal reward the target is the Monte Carlo return and the critic is only a baseline. Group-based methods (Section 5) replace it by the mean reward of $K$ responses, a Monte Carlo value estimate for the prompt.
- **Process reward models** (Section 5). Labelling a reasoning prefix with the success rate of rollouts continued from it is a Monte Carlo estimate of $V^\pi$ there — with a binary terminal reward and $\gamma = 1$, $V^\pi(s)$ is the probability of eventual success — so a PRM is a learned value function, with the usual weaknesses of one.
- **Critics for real-robot RL** (Section 7). Sample-efficient real-world RL uses off-policy actor–critic learners with replay buffers seeded by demonstrations: the machinery of DQN, TD3 and SAC.
- **Q-functions steering a frozen policy** (Section 7). Executing the best of several chunks sampled from a frozen VLA, by a learned $Q$, restricts the maximum to actions the policy finds plausible — the simplest guard against extrapolation error. Backpropagating a critic through a sampler, also described there, is a deterministic policy gradient in the style of DDPG.

Whether TD methods work at the scale of large pretrained models — accurate token-level critics, stable bootstrapping in very large networks, offline RL on logged interactions — is open. Large-scale practice for language models mostly avoids learned critics; robotics leans on them.

Section 3 takes the other route to control: instead of learning $Q^\ast$ and maximizing it, parametrize the policy and follow the gradient of $J(\theta)$. The value function returns there as a critic, trained by exactly the TD($\lambda$) machinery of this section.
