Sections 1 and 2 built reinforcement learning around value functions: estimate $Q^\pi$ or $Q^*$, then act greedily. This section takes the other route. We write down a parametric policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$, regard the expected return $J(\theta)$ as a function of $\theta$, and climb its gradient. Turning that idea into a working algorithm takes three ingredients: a way to estimate the gradient from samples, a way to make the estimate less noisy, and a way to take large steps without wrecking the policy. The end point is **proximal policy optimization (PPO)**, the starting point for almost everything in Sections 4–9.

The section closes with KL-regularized RL and its closed-form optimal policy. That formula is behind direct preference optimization (Section 4) and its diffusion-model counterpart (Section 9), so it is derived carefully.

## Why optimize the policy directly

Value-based methods need $\max_a Q(s, a)$, both to act and to form Bellman targets. That is easy for a handful of discrete actions and awkward otherwise. Policy-based methods avoid the maximization, which matters in four situations.

- **Continuous actions.** Maximizing a learned $Q(s, \cdot)$ over $\mathbb{R}^d$ at every step is an inner optimization problem; a Gaussian policy simply samples.
- **Huge structured action spaces.** A language model's response is a sequence of tokens from a large vocabulary. Responses cannot be enumerated, but they can be sampled.
- **Stochastic policies.** Under partial observability the best policy can be genuinely random, and a policy required to stay close to a given distribution (Section 4) is random by design. Greedy policies are deterministic.
- **A pretrained model is already a policy.** A language model defines $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t})$ by a softmax; a diffusion model defines Gaussian transitions $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, c)$; a vision-language-action model outputs a distribution over actions. Each can be sampled and each has a computable log-probability. Policy gradient methods need exactly these two operations, so they fine-tune the pretrained model in place.

The last point is why this section matters for the rest of the notes: in every later section the policy is a large pretrained generative model, and RL adjusts it rather than discovering behaviour from nothing.

<Definition name="Policy optimization problem">
Fix an MDP $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma, \mu)$ and policies $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ differentiable in $\theta$. A trajectory $\tau = (s_0, a_0, r_0, s_1, \dots)$ is generated by $s_0 \sim \mu$, $a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)$, $s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t)$, with $r_t = r(s_t, a_t)$. The **objective** is
$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t r_t \right] = \mathbb{E}_{s_0 \sim \mu}\left[ V^{\pi_\theta}(s_0) \right].
$$
Throughout, either $\gamma \lt 1$ and rewards are bounded, or episodes end at a finite time $T$, so every sum converges.
</Definition>

## The policy gradient

### The score function trick

The parameter $\theta$ enters $J$ only through the distribution of $\tau$. The **log-derivative** (or **score function**) **trick** moves the gradient onto that distribution. For densities $p_\theta$ and a function $f$ not depending on $\theta$,
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_\theta}[f(z)] = \int f(z)\, \nabla_\theta p_\theta(z)\, dz = \int f(z)\, p_\theta(z)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(z)\, dz = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_\theta}\left[ f(z)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(z) \right],
$$
using $\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta$ and assuming differentiation under the integral is permitted (for instance, $f$ bounded and $\nabla_\theta p_\theta$ dominated by an integrable function). The right-hand side can be estimated by sampling, and $f$ need not be differentiable or even known in closed form; only $\log p_\theta$ must be differentiable.

Take $z = \tau$. The trajectory density factors as $p_\theta(\tau) = \mu(s_0) \prod_{t} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$, so
$$
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_{t \ge 0} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t).
$$
The initial distribution and the dynamics drop out because they do not depend on $\theta$: **the gradient can be estimated without a model of the environment.**

<Result name="Policy gradient, trajectory form">
With $R(\tau) = \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t r_t$,
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ R(\tau) \sum_{t \ge 0} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right].
$$
</Result>

<Example>

For a **softmax policy** with logits $z = z_\theta(s)$, $\nabla_z \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = e_a - \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)$: raising $\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ raises the logit of $a$ and lowers the others in proportion to their probability. A language model's next-token distribution has this form. For a **Gaussian policy** $\mathcal{N}(m_\theta(s), \sigma^2 I)$, $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \sigma^{-2} (\nabla_\theta m_\theta(s))^\top (a - m_\theta(s))$, which moves the mean towards rewarded actions. Each step of a stochastic diffusion sampler is a Gaussian of this kind (Section 9).

</Example>

Averaging the bracket over $N$ sampled trajectories gives an unbiased gradient estimate; stochastic gradient ascent with it is **REINFORCE** (Williams, 1992). It is correct but very noisy: every action is credited with the whole return, including rewards received before it was taken. The next section removes two sources of noise without introducing bias.

The score function estimator is not the only way to differentiate an expectation. When samples can be written as $z = g_\theta(\varepsilon)$ with independent noise $\varepsilon$ and $f$ is differentiable, the **pathwise** (reparametrization) estimator $\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta f(g_\theta(\varepsilon))]$ usually has much lower variance. In RL the environment and reward are rarely differentiable, so it is seldom available; Section 9 returns to the contrast, since a differentiable reward model makes both options possible.

## Causality and baselines

### Reward-to-go

The basic fact is that the score has mean zero:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \right] = \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = 0
$$
(an integral for continuous actions). Fix $t$ and $k \lt t$. The reward $r_k$ is a function of the history $h_t = (s_0, a_0, \dots, s_t)$, so conditioning on $h_t$ gives
$$
\mathbb{E}\left[ r_k\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right] = \mathbb{E}\left[ r_k\, \mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)}\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right] \right] = 0.
$$
Dropping these terms leaves only later rewards, and $\sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^k r_k = \gamma^t G_t$:
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t\, G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right].
$$
The **reward-to-go** $G_t$ replaces the full return. In practice the factor $\gamma^t$ is usually dropped; the resulting update is no longer exactly the gradient of $J$ but a widely used heuristic. With $\gamma = 1$ and finite episodes, the usual setting for language models, the issue does not arise.

### Baselines

<Result name="Baselines are unbiased">
Let $b(s_t)$ depend only on the state (and possibly on $t$), not on $a_t$ or anything later. Then $\mathbb{E}[ b(s_t)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) ] = 0$, and therefore
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \left( G_t - b(s_t) \right) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right].
$$
</Result>

*Proof.* Conditioning on $s_t$, the factor $b(s_t)$ is fixed and comes out of the inner expectation, leaving $b(s_t)\, \mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)] = 0$. Take the outer expectation. $\square$

The argument fails if $b$ depends on $a_t$, because then it cannot be pulled out. This is why it matters in Section 5 that a group baseline is computed from *other* samples than the one it is applied to.

A baseline leaves the mean unchanged but can reduce the variance a great deal. If all returns are large and positive, the raw estimator raises the probability of every sampled action and relies on cancellation between samples; subtracting the typical return makes good actions positive and bad ones negative. The natural choice is $b(s) = V^\pi(s)$. It is not exactly variance-optimal, but it is close in practice and leads to the advantage.

## The policy gradient theorem

Since $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ depends only on $(s_t, a_t)$, the tower property lets us replace $G_t$ by $\mathbb{E}[G_t \mid s_t, a_t] = Q^\pi(s_t, a_t)$. With the baseline $V^\pi$, this becomes the advantage $A^\pi = Q^\pi - V^\pi$. For $\gamma \lt 1$ define the **discounted state visitation distribution** $d^\pi(s) = (1 - \gamma) \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \Pr\nolimits_\pi(s_t = s)$.

<Result name="Policy gradient theorem (Sutton et al., 2000)">
For $\gamma \lt 1$,
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \frac{1}{1 - \gamma}\, \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_\theta},\, a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\left[ Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \right] = \frac{1}{1 - \gamma}\, \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_\theta},\, a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\left[ A^{\pi_\theta}(s, a)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \right].
$$
</Result>

The content of the theorem is what is absent: no derivative of the state distribution $d^\pi$ appears, although changing $\theta$ changes which states are visited. The advantage form says: **raise the log-probability of each action in proportion to how much better it is than the policy's average action in that state.** Every method in these notes is an instance of this sentence; they differ in how they estimate $A^\pi$ and how far a single update may move.

## Actor–critic methods and GAE

The Monte Carlo estimate $\hat A_t = G_t - V(s_t)$ is unbiased for any state-dependent $V$, but its variance grows with the horizon. An **actor–critic** method trains a **critic** $V_\psi(s) \approx V^\pi(s)$ and uses it to replace parts of the return by predictions. The building block is the **temporal-difference residual**
$$
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\psi(s_{t+1}) - V_\psi(s_t).
$$
If $V_\psi = V^\pi$ exactly, then $\mathbb{E}[\delta_t \mid s_t, a_t] = Q^\pi(s_t, a_t) - V^\pi(s_t) = A^\pi(s_t, a_t)$: an unbiased estimate involving a single random reward. With an imperfect critic it is biased. In between lie the **$k$-step estimates**, which telescope:
$$
\hat A_t^{(k)} = \sum_{l = 0}^{k - 1} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l} = -V_\psi(s_t) + r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \dots + \gamma^{k-1} r_{t+k-1} + \gamma^k V_\psi(s_{t+k}).
$$
Small $k$ trusts the critic (low variance, more bias); large $k$ trusts the sampled rewards. As $k \to \infty$, or when $t + k$ passes the end of the episode (where $V_\psi = 0$), $\hat A_t^{(k)} \to G_t - V_\psi(s_t)$.

<Definition name="Generalized advantage estimation">
For $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, the GAE estimator (Schulman et al., 2016) is the geometric average of the $k$-step estimates:
$$
\hat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma, \lambda)} = (1 - \lambda) \sum_{k \ge 1} \lambda^{k-1} \hat A_t^{(k)} = \sum_{l \ge 0} (\gamma \lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}.
$$
</Definition>

The second equality is an exchange of summation: substituting $\hat A_t^{(k)} = \sum_{l \lt k} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l}$,
$$
(1 - \lambda) \sum_{k \ge 1} \lambda^{k-1} \sum_{l = 0}^{k-1} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l} = \sum_{l \ge 0} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l}\, (1 - \lambda) \sum_{k \ge l + 1} \lambda^{k-1} = \sum_{l \ge 0} (\gamma \lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}
$$
for $\lambda \lt 1$; the right-hand side also makes sense at $\lambda = 1$ and is taken as the definition there. In code it is computed backwards by $\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma \lambda\, \hat A_{t+1}$, starting from zero after the last step, and the critic is fitted to the **$\lambda$-return** $\hat A_t + V_\psi(s_t)$.

At $\lambda = 0$ the estimate is $\delta_t$: lowest variance, fully reliant on the critic. At $\lambda = 1$ it is $G_t - V_\psi(s_t)$: the critic is only a baseline, so the estimate is unbiased however poor the critic, at the price of the highest variance. Intermediate $\lambda$ trades between them. With $\gamma = \lambda = 1$ and a single reward at the end — a language model scored once per response — every token gets $\hat A_t = r(x, y) - V_\psi(s_t)$, the final reward minus the critic's prediction at that token; $\lambda \lt 1$ spreads credit using differences of predictions along the response, which helps only if the critic is accurate at token level. Note that $\gamma$ plays two roles: it defines the objective, but $\gamma \lt 1$ inside GAE is often used purely to reduce variance even when the true objective is undiscounted.

## Off-policy correction and surrogate objectives

### Importance sampling

Collecting fresh trajectories for every gradient step is expensive, so we would like to take several steps on data collected by an earlier policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$. Importance sampling, $\mathbb{E}_p[f] = \mathbb{E}_q[(p/q) f]$, applied to whole trajectories gives the weight $\prod_t \rho_t(\theta)$ with the per-step ratio
$$
\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)},
$$
the dynamics again cancelling. This is unbiased but useless for long horizons. Each factor has conditional mean 1 and conditional second moment $1 + \chi^2(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot \mid s_t))$; conditioning backwards from the last step, if every per-state $\chi^2$ divergence is at least $c$, the second moment of the product over $T$ steps is at least $(1 + c)^T$. The variance is exponential in the horizon. For a response of hundreds of tokens, even a tiny per-token change gives weights that are nearly all close to zero, with rare enormous values.

### The surrogate objective

Practical methods use per-step ratios only, at the cost of an approximation controlled by an exact identity.

<Result name="Performance-difference lemma (Kakade and Langford, 2002)">
For any two policies $\pi, \pi'$,
$$
J(\pi') - J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi'}\left[ \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t A^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) \right] = \frac{1}{1 - \gamma}\, \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi'},\, a \sim \pi'(\cdot \mid s)}\left[ A^\pi(s, a) \right].
$$
</Result>

*Proof.* Along any trajectory $\sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t ( \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) - V^\pi(s_t) ) = -V^\pi(s_0)$ by telescoping. Hence, since both policies share $\mu$,
$$
\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi'}\left[ \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \left( r_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) - V^\pi(s_t) \right) \right] = J(\pi') - \mathbb{E}_{s_0 \sim \mu}[V^\pi(s_0)] = J(\pi') - J(\pi).
$$
Conditioning each summand on $(s_t, a_t)$, and using that the dynamics do not depend on the policy, $\mathbb{E}[r_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) \mid s_t, a_t] = Q^\pi(s_t, a_t)$, so the summand becomes $A^\pi(s_t, a_t)$. $\square$

The lemma needs states from the *new* policy, which we do not have. The **surrogate objective** keeps the old policy's states and corrects only the action:
$$
L_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{1 - \gamma}\, \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}},\, a \sim \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot \mid s)}\left[ \rho(\theta)\, A^{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}(s, a) \right].
$$
At $\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ it equals $0$ (since $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[A^\pi(s, a)] = 0$) and its gradient equals $\nabla_\theta J(\theta_{\mathrm{old}})$ (since $\nabla_\theta \rho = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$ there, recovering the policy gradient theorem). So it matches $J(\theta) - J(\theta_{\mathrm{old}})$ to first order. Further away the state distributions differ and the surrogate can be badly wrong; Schulman et al. (2015) bounded the error by a multiple of the maximum over states of $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s))$. Maximizing the surrogate is safe only where the policy changes little.

## Trust regions: the natural gradient and TRPO

Ordinary gradient ascent bounds the step in parameter space, which is the wrong geometry: a small change in some parameters can change the policy drastically, a large change in others hardly at all. What should be bounded is the change in the *distribution*. To second order,
$$
\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left( \pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_{\theta + d}(\cdot \mid s) \right) \right] \approx \tfrac12\, d^\top F(\theta)\, d, \qquad F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,\, a \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta^\top \right],
$$
the **Fisher information matrix** averaged over states. Maximizing $\nabla_\theta J^\top d$ subject to $\tfrac12 d^\top F d \le \delta$ gives, by a Lagrange multiplier, a step along $F(\theta)^{-1} \nabla_\theta J(\theta)$, the **natural policy gradient** (Kakade, 2001). It depends only on the distributions the parameters define, not on the parametrization.

**Trust region policy optimization** (TRPO; Schulman et al., 2015) approximately solves
$$
\max_\theta\ L_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\theta) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\left( \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s) \right) \right] \le \delta,
$$
computing the natural-gradient direction by conjugate gradient with Fisher–vector products (never forming $F$), then a backtracking line search that checks the actual surrogate and KL. It is reliable but needs second-order machinery and fits awkwardly with parameters shared between policy and critic. PPO was designed to keep its behaviour using first-order optimization only.

## Proximal policy optimization

### The clipped objective

**PPO** (Schulman et al., 2017) replaces the KL constraint by a surrogate that removes the incentive to move the ratio far from 1.

<Definition name="PPO clipped objective">
With an advantage estimate $\hat A_t$ (usually GAE) and clip range $\epsilon$ (typically around $0.2$),
$$
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\left[ \min\left( \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}\left( \rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon \right) \hat A_t \right) \right],
$$
where $\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t$ averages over a batch of time steps collected with $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$.
</Definition>

The min and the clip are easy to misread, so take the two signs of the advantage separately.

**If $\hat A_t \gt 0$** (we want $\rho_t$ to grow), factor out $\hat A_t$: the term is $\hat A_t \min(\rho_t, \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t))$. For $\rho_t \lt 1 - \epsilon$ the clipped value is larger, so the min is $\rho_t$; inside the range both agree; above $1 + \epsilon$ the min is $1 + \epsilon$. So the term equals $\hat A_t \min(\rho_t, 1 + \epsilon)$: increasing the action's probability pays off up to a ratio of $1 + \epsilon$ and then the objective is flat.

**If $\hat A_t \lt 0$** (we want $\rho_t$ to shrink), factoring out the negative $\hat A_t$ turns the min into a max, and the term equals $\hat A_t \max(\rho_t, 1 - \epsilon)$: decreasing the probability pays off down to $1 - \epsilon$, then the objective is flat.

| | $\rho_t \lt 1 - \epsilon$ | $1 - \epsilon \le \rho_t \le 1 + \epsilon$ | $\rho_t \gt 1 + \epsilon$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\hat A_t \gt 0$ | $\rho_t \hat A_t$, gradient active | $\rho_t \hat A_t$, gradient active | $(1+\epsilon)\hat A_t$, gradient zero |
| $\hat A_t \lt 0$ | $(1-\epsilon)\hat A_t$, gradient zero | $\rho_t \hat A_t$, gradient active | $\rho_t \hat A_t$, gradient active |

In words: **a sample stops contributing gradient once the policy has moved more than $\epsilon$ in the direction its advantage asks for; it never stops contributing when the policy has moved the wrong way.** $L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}$ is a pessimistic lower bound on the unclipped surrogate, equal to it near $\rho_t = 1$, with the ordinary policy gradient at $\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{old}}$.

<Remark>

Clipping removes the incentive to leave $[1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon]$ but does not enforce it: updates driven by other samples through shared parameters can still push ratios outside, and over several epochs the KL to $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ can exceed what $\epsilon$ suggests. Implementations often monitor the KL and stop early. The PPO paper also describes a variant with an adaptive KL penalty in place of clipping.

</Remark>

### The full loss and the training loop

With a shared or jointly trained critic, PPO maximizes
$$
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\left[ \min\left( \rho_t \hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\, \hat A_t \right) - c_1 \left( V_\psi(s_t) - \hat V_t^{\mathrm{targ}} \right)^2 + c_2\, \mathcal{H}\left( \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t) \right) \right]:
$$
the clipped surrogate, a **value loss** towards the $\lambda$-return targets $\hat V_t^{\mathrm{targ}} = \hat A_t + V_{\psi_{\mathrm{old}}}(s_t)$, and an **entropy bonus** $\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot \mid s)) = -\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \log \pi(a \mid s)$ that discourages premature collapse to a deterministic policy. Advantages are usually normalized to zero mean and unit variance per minibatch.

```
PPO (actor-critic, clipped objective)
initialize policy theta, critic psi
repeat:
    theta_old <- theta
    # 1. rollout
    run pi_theta_old to collect N steps; store s_t, a_t, r_t,
        log pi_theta_old(a_t | s_t), V_psi(s_t)
    # 2. advantages, backwards in t within each trajectory
    delta_t = r_t + gamma * V_psi(s_t+1) - V_psi(s_t)     (V = 0 after termination)
    A_t     = delta_t + gamma * lambda * A_t+1
    Vtarg_t = A_t + V_psi(s_t)
    normalize A over the batch
    # 3. several epochs of minibatch updates on the same batch
    for epoch in 1..E, for each minibatch:
        ratio  = exp(log pi_theta(a_t | s_t) - log pi_theta_old(a_t | s_t))
        policy = mean( min(ratio * A_t, clip(ratio, 1-eps, 1+eps) * A_t) )
        value  = mean( (V_psi(s_t) - Vtarg_t)^2 )
        ent    = mean( entropy of pi_theta(. | s_t) )
        gradient step on (theta, psi) to decrease  -policy + c1*value - c2*ent
    optionally stop early if mean KL(pi_theta_old || pi_theta) exceeds a target
```

The first epoch on a batch is essentially an on-policy gradient step; later epochs reuse the data, which is where clipping earns its keep. The per-iteration cost is that of ordinary first-order training. Performance depends noticeably on implementation details such as advantage normalization, learning-rate schedules and value-loss handling, a point made carefully by Engstrom et al. (2020).

## KL-regularized RL

### The objective and its optimum

From Section 4 on, the policy starts as a pretrained model $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ that is already good at most of what it does. Pure reward maximization tends to destroy that, drifting towards whatever exploits the reward. The standard remedy penalizes divergence from the reference. We state it in the contextual-bandit form used for language models: a prompt $x \sim \mathcal{D}$, a whole response $y$ as the action, and a reward $r(x, y)$ at the end.

<Definition name="KL-regularized objective">
For $\beta \gt 0$,
$$
J_\beta(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\left[ r(x, y) \right] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\left( \pi(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x) \right) \right].
$$
</Definition>

<Result name="Optimal KL-regularized policy">
Assume $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp(r(x, y)/\beta)$ is finite for each $x$ (automatic if $r$ is bounded; an integral for continuous $y$). Over all policies, $J_\beta$ is maximized, uniquely for each $x$ up to sets of probability zero, by
$$
\pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\left( \frac{r(x, y)}{\beta} \right),
$$
and the optimal value for prompt $x$ is $\beta \log Z(x)$.
</Result>

*Proof.* The objective separates over $x$; fix $x$ and drop it from the notation. A policy with infinite KL cannot be optimal, so assume $\pi$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Combining the two terms,
$$
J_\beta(\pi) = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\left[ \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)} - \frac{r(y)}{\beta} \right] = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\left[ \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}} \right].
$$
The denominator equals $Z\, \pi^*(y)$, so
$$
J_\beta(\pi) = -\beta\, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\left[ \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^*(y)} \right] + \beta \log Z = \beta \log Z - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*).
$$
The first term does not depend on $\pi$, and $\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*) \ge 0$ with equality exactly when $\pi = \pi^*$. $\square$

This is the **Gibbs variational principle**, $\log \mathbb{E}_q[e^f] = \max_p ( \mathbb{E}_p[f] - \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) )$ with maximizer $p \propto q\, e^f$, applied with $f = r/\beta$ and $q = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Three consequences recur later.

- **The reward is determined by the optimal policy up to a prompt-dependent constant:**
$$
r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
$$
The term $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels when two responses to the same prompt are compared. This is the starting point of DPO (Section 4) and Diffusion-DPO (Section 9).
- **The optimum reweights; it does not invent.** $\pi^*$ is $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ tilted by $e^{r/\beta}$, so responses with zero reference probability stay at zero. As $\beta \to \infty$, $\pi^* \to \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$; as $\beta \to 0$ it concentrates on the reward-maximizing responses in the support of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.
- **Nothing depends on what $y$ is.** The argument applies equally to token sequences, robot trajectories, or a whole denoising chain $x_T, \dots, x_0$.

The result describes the optimum over *all* policies; a parametrized $\pi_\theta$ may not represent $\pi^*$, and an optimizer may not find the best approximation.

### Imposing the KL in practice

By the chain rule for KL divergence, with $s_t = (x, y_{\lt t})$,
$$
\mathrm{KL}\left( \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x) \right) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_t \mid s_t)} \right].
$$
There are two common ways to use this.

1. **In the reward.** Give token $t$ the reward $-\beta \log(\pi_\theta(y_t \mid s_t)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_t \mid s_t))$, plus $r(x, y)$ at the last token, and run ordinary PPO; the expected undiscounted return is exactly $J_\beta$ for that prompt. The log-ratio is computed with the sampling policy and treated as a fixed reward, so the penalty enters through the advantages. This is the standard RLHF arrangement of Section 4.
2. **As a loss term.** Keep the reward $r(x, y)$ and add $\beta$ times a per-token KL estimate on the sampled tokens directly to the loss, as GRPO does (Section 5). The KL gradient then comes from differentiating the estimator, so the choice of estimator matters.

With $y \sim \pi$ and $u = \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)/\pi(y)$, three single-sample estimators of $\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ are common, popularized by a note of Schulman's: $k_1 = -\log u$ (unbiased, high variance, often negative), $k_2 = \tfrac12 (\log u)^2$ (biased, non-negative, low variance when $\pi \approx \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$) and $k_3 = (u - 1) - \log u$ (unbiased since $\mathbb{E}_\pi[u] = 1$, and non-negative since $\log u \le u - 1$).

<Remark>

An unbiased *value* does not guarantee the right *gradient* when the estimator is differentiated with samples held fixed. For a single distribution and on-policy samples, direct computation gives
$$
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta k_1] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta k_2] = \nabla_\theta \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}), \qquad \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta k_3] = \nabla_\theta \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta).
$$
For instance $\nabla_\theta k_3 = (1 - u) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$, whose expectation is $-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta]$: the gradient of the KL with its arguments swapped, not of the one being estimated. At the sequence level further terms from the changing state distribution appear. How much these discrepancies matter in practice is still being worked out.

</Remark>

### Entropy regularization

If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is uniform on a finite action set, $\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \log |\mathcal{A}| - \mathcal{H}(\pi)$, so an entropy bonus $\alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi)$ is KL-regularization towards uniform with $\beta = \alpha$, and the optimum is the Boltzmann policy $\pi^*(a) \propto \exp(r(a)/\alpha)$. With the bonus added at every step of a sequential problem this becomes **maximum-entropy RL**, the framework behind soft actor–critic. In LLM fine-tuning, where the reference is the pretrained model, explicit entropy bonuses are less common, and the collapse of entropy during RL is itself an object of study (Section 5).

## Practical issues and research directions

PPO with a KL penalty is the baseline that later methods are measured against, and most of them respond to one of its weaknesses.

- **Sample efficiency.** PPO is on-policy: each batch is used for a few epochs and discarded, which dominates the cost when rollouts are expensive. Large systems often also generate rollouts with a separate inference engine whose log-probabilities differ slightly from the trainer's, so nominally on-policy data is mildly off-policy; how best to correct for this is an active question.
- **The cost of the critic.** For a language model the critic is typically a network comparable in size to the policy, roughly doubling memory and compute. It must predict the final reward from a partial response, a hard regression problem, and a poor critic injects bias through GAE. This motivates the critic-free methods of Section 5, which replace $V_\psi$ by an average over $K$ sampled responses $y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(K)}$ to the same prompt.
- **Credit assignment with sparse rewards.** With one scalar at the end of a long response and $\gamma = \lambda = 1$, every token receives the same signal up to the baseline. Finer credit needs an accurate critic or denser rewards; process rewards (Section 5) and step-level rewards in robotics (Section 7) pursue the latter.
- **Over-optimization.** The policy optimizes the reward it is given, not the one intended. The KL penalty slows exploitation of a learned reward model but does not prevent it, and choosing $\beta$ is largely empirical (Section 4).
- **What the clip really does.** The trust-region motivation is clean, but the clipped objective is a heuristic whose success depends on implementation choices. Asymmetric clipping, sequence-level ratios and other constraints all appear in later sections; which choices matter, and why, is not settled.

Section 4 puts these pieces together for language models: a reward model learned from human comparisons, KL-regularized PPO to optimize it, and the closed-form optimal policy above turned into a loss that needs no reinforcement learning at all.
